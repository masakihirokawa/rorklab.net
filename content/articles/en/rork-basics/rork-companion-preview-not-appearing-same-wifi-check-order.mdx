One evening I opened Companion, scanned the QR code, and waited about ten minutes. The screen stayed white. No error, no spinner that went anywhere.

The Wi-Fi icon was there. The same project was running fine in my browser. Nothing was reaching the phone.

What I did first was restart the router. Then I deleted and reinstalled Companion. Neither helped. It was only when I opened the Settings app that I found it: the Local Network permission had been turned off.

That cost me about fifteen minutes, and the lesson wasn't about networking at all. It was about order. **The harder something is to undo, the later it belongs in the sequence.** Restarts and reinstalls now sit at the very end of my list.

## Before blaming the Wi-Fi, try cellular alone

I should start with something I haven't been able to pin down. Whether Companion's preview travels directly between devices inside your home, or arrives by way of Rork's own servers, isn't something I've found stated plainly in the official docs. So I use an order of checks that gives a useful answer either way.

The very first thing I do is turn Wi-Fi off on the iPhone and scan the same QR code over cellular only. Nothing changes on the computer.

<table>
<tr><th>Test</th><th>If the preview appears</th><th>If it doesn't</th></tr>
<tr><td>iPhone on cellular only</td><td>The path doesn't need your home network, so router settings become an unlikely suspect</td><td>Either the path needs the local network, or the problem is on the device or account side</td></tr>
<tr><td>Computer joined to the iPhone's Personal Hotspot</td><td>The phone and Companion are fine together; the home router is the suspect</td><td>Device settings or the account</td></tr>
<tr><td>Same home Wi-Fi, a different iPhone or iPad</td><td>Something specific to the first phone</td><td>The project itself, or the home network path</td></tr>
</table>

If you're unsure which to try first, **start with whichever is easiest to put back.** For me that's the cellular test, because it's one toggle. I only move on to the hotspot when cellular doesn't show anything. Both take a couple of minutes to undo, which feels far less risky than opening the router's admin page.

## "Nothing appears" and "it stops after a while" are different problems

Both feel like "it won't connect," but they happen in different places. If you don't separate them first, you can't tell whether something you did actually fixed it.

<table>
<tr><th>What you see</th><th>Layer to suspect</th><th>First place to look</th></tr>
<tr><td>QR scans, screen stays blank</td><td>The path between device and router</td><td>Local Network permission</td></tr>
<tr><td>It loads, then dies a few minutes in</td><td>Power management on the phone</td><td>Low Power Mode, auto-lock</td></tr>
<tr><td>Works on the Mac, not on this iPhone</td><td>Something specific to that phone</td><td>Private Wi-Fi Address</td></tr>
<tr><td>Fails at home, works elsewhere</td><td>Router-side isolation</td><td>Guest network, AP isolation</td></tr>
</table>

Mixing up the first two rows and restarting over and over was the slowest path I found. Now I say it out loud before touching anything: blank means routing, dies later means power.

## The whole thing as one table

Here's everything above folded into a single table, in the order I'd actually work through it. Rows near the top are faster to check and easier to reverse.

<table>
<tr><th>Symptom</th><th>Likely causes</th><th>How to confirm</th><th>Fix</th></tr>
<tr><td>QR scans, nothing ever appears</td><td>Local Network permission is off</td><td>Settings &gt; Privacy &amp; Security &gt; Local Network: is Companion listed and on?</td><td>Turn it on, force-quit Companion, scan again</td></tr>
<tr><td>Fails at home, works on cellular</td><td>AP isolation, guest SSID</td><td>Compare the network name on both devices, character by character</td><td>Join the same SSID; disable isolation while testing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Works on the Mac, fails on this iPhone</td><td>Private Wi-Fi Address, VPN or a configuration profile</td><td>Scan the same QR from another iPhone or iPad</td><td>Set the address to Fixed for that network; disconnect VPN while testing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Loads, then stops a few minutes later</td><td>Low Power Mode, auto-lock</td><td>Leave the screen on for five minutes and see if it still stops</td><td>Turn off Low Power Mode and lengthen auto-lock during testing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Companion errors before you even scan</td><td>Outdated Companion, signed into a different account</td><td>Check App Store updates and compare accounts with the web editor</td><td>Update, then sign in with the same account</td></tr>
<tr><td>Blank on every network and every device</td><td>A generation error in the project</td><td>Look for errors in the Rork editor</td><td>Fix the error, then reopen the preview</td></tr>
</table>

That last row is worth taking seriously. If you land there, it isn't a network problem at all, and no amount of fiddling with the phone will change it. Going back to the editor was always the shortest way out for me.

## Split the problem in half in one minute

Before you start doubting your home network, try once without it. Turn on Personal Hotspot on the iPhone, connect your computer to that hotspot, and scan the same QR code again.

If the preview appears, the phone and Companion are fine together, and the problem lives in your home router. If it still doesn't appear, it's the device or the account side.

That single step cuts roughly half the search space. It does use mobile data, so switch back as soon as you have your answer.

It's worth being strict about what counts as a result here. A preview that appears and then immediately stalls is not the same as one that never arrives, and on a hotspot the signal can be weak enough to muddy the difference. I wait for the first screen to render and respond to a tap before I call it a pass.

I write the result down, one line, right then. When the same symptom shows up the following week, I don't trust myself to remember which half it was last time.

## Four things to check on the iPhone

If the device side is implicated, I go through Settings from the top. All four of these can end up in a state you never chose deliberately.

<table>
<tr><th>Where to look</th><th>What it does</th><th>How to undo it</th></tr>
<tr><td>Privacy &amp; Security &gt; Local Network</td><td>Blocks discovery of other devices on the same Wi-Fi</td><td>Switch Companion back on</td></tr>
<tr><td>Wi-Fi &gt; current network &gt; Private Wi-Fi Address</td><td>Rotates the device identifier, so router allowlists stop matching</td><td>Set it to Fixed for that network only</td></tr>
<tr><td>Battery &gt; Low Power Mode</td><td>Throttles activity once the screen goes dark</td><td>Turn it off while you're testing</td></tr>
<tr><td>General &gt; VPN &amp; Device Management</td><td>Can route traffic somewhere you didn't expect</td><td>Disconnect for the duration of the test</td></tr>
</table>

The second row deserves a word too, because it fails in a way that looks random. Private Wi-Fi Address is set per network, and it rotates. If your router has any kind of allowlist or reserved-address rule tied to the phone, the match quietly stops holding and the symptom arrives days after the change that caused it.

The first row is the one I hit most often. iOS asks for that permission exactly once, and if you tap Don't Allow, the prompt never comes back. Nothing on screen tells you why the preview is missing, so unless you know where the switch lives, there's no trail to follow.

A note on iCloud Private Relay, since it comes up: it mainly covers Safari and some unencrypted traffic, and it doesn't sit between two devices on the same Wi-Fi. I'd only add it to the list if there's a managed configuration profile from work or school on the phone.

## Two things on the router side

If the phone comes back clean, look at the house. There isn't much to check — realistically, two things.

The first is AP isolation. Depending on the manufacturer it might be called Privacy Separator, client isolation, or something about blocking device-to-device traffic. When it's on, two devices joined to the same SSID can't see each other at all. Guest networks often ship with it enabled, which is how I lost an afternoon once.

The second is simply being on a different network than you think. If your 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands have separate SSIDs, your laptop and your phone may be on different names. Mesh setups can do the same thing when a device latches onto a different node. Phones are especially good at quietly reconnecting to whichever access point had the stronger signal when you last walked through the house.

The quickest way to rule this out is to read the network name on both devices and compare them character for character, rather than trusting that they're "on the Wi-Fi." Twice now I've found a trailing -5G I'd never noticed, on a network I set up myself.

<table>
<tr><th>Setting</th><th>Common label</th><th>What to do while testing</th></tr>
<tr><td>Device-to-device blocking</td><td>AP isolation / Privacy Separator</td><td>Turn off temporarily</td></tr>
<tr><td>Visitor network</td><td>Guest SSID</td><td>Don't use it for testing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Per-band SSID split</td><td>Names ending in -A or -G</td><td>Put both devices on the same one</td></tr>
</table>

I've rarely had something survive all of that. If it does, that's the moment to restart the router and reinstall Companion — last, not first, because those are the steps that erase the evidence you'd need to understand what happened.

## What you learn here comes back in your own app

There's a second half to this detour. The Local Network permission isn't a Companion quirk. Your own app needs it too, the moment it tries to find anything else on the same network.

In Expo you declare the purpose in `app.json`. Without it, iOS never shows the permission prompt at all — your discovery code simply finds nothing.

```json
{
  "expo": {
    "ios": {
      "infoPlist": {
        "NSLocalNetworkUsageDescription": "Used to find speakers on your Wi-Fi network.",
        "NSBonjourServices": ["_myapp._tcp"]
      }
    }
  }
}
```

`NSLocalNetworkUsageDescription` is the sentence the user reads in the prompt. `NSBonjourServices` lists the service types you intend to browse for; anything you forget to declare stays invisible even after permission is granted.

One thing I've grown careful about is the wording. Concrete purposes get accepted far more often than vague ones. "Used for communication" gives the reader nothing to decide with. And as we just saw, this prompt appears once — after a decline, the only way back is buried in Settings. So: **a prompt you get one shot at deserves a sentence that lands on the first read.**

I've rewritten those strings in my own published apps more than once. The first version was almost always written for me rather than for the person holding the phone.

### Confirming the strings actually made it into the build

Writing them in `app.json` isn't proof. If the key sits one level off, the build still succeeds and the Info.plist simply doesn't contain it. If you have the code exported locally, generate the native project and look.

```bash
# Generate the ios/ folder (use --clean to regenerate if it already exists)
npx expo prebuild --platform ios --clean

# Show only the local-network entries from the generated Info.plist (macOS)
plutil -p ios/*/Info.plist | grep -A2 -E "NSLocalNetworkUsageDescription|NSBonjourServices"
```

When it's right, you'll see both keys:

```
  "NSBonjourServices" => [
    0 => "_myapp._tcp"
  ]
  "NSLocalNetworkUsageDescription" => "Used to find speakers on your Wi-Fi network."
```

If nothing prints, check that `infoPlist` sits inside `ios`. The mistake I made once was putting it directly under `expo`. The config still loads without complaint, which is exactly why it took me a while to notice.

## Three things people often get wrong

**"If the Wi-Fi icon is showing, both devices are on the same network."** The icon only tells you the phone is on *some* Wi-Fi. In homes with per-band SSIDs or mesh nodes, your laptop and phone can easily be on different names. What you want to confirm is that the names match.

**"If I tap Don't Allow, it'll just ask me again next time."** It won't. The Local Network prompt appears once per app. Apple's technote TN3179 describes the user's choice as something managed from Settings, and Settings is the only place to reverse it.

**"Once the usage string is there, discovery will work."** For Bonjour you also need to list the service types in `NSBonjourServices`. Leave a type out and you get the most confusing state of all: permission granted, results empty.

The setting names and paths here reflect iOS as of November 2026. They shift a little between versions, so if you can't find the screen, typing "Local Network" into the search field in Settings is usually fastest.

## One thing to do today

Open Settings, go to Privacy & Security, and tap Local Network. Check that Companion is listed and switched on. That's the whole task.

The next time you're staring at a blank preview, that memory will shorten the first minute. For me it's been the difference between fifteen minutes and one.
