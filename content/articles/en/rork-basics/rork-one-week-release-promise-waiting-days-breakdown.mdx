I was writing an update note for one of my wallpaper apps when I stalled on the date field. The work itself was two days from done. Even so, I couldn't make myself type "next week."

I saved the note with the date left blank. That was the night I realized the waiting had never been part of my estimate, because none of it was in my hands.

Once a Rork app takes shape, it's easy to feel that the only thing left is to ship. For a first release, though, **the waiting often takes longer than the building.**

The thing I'd like to say up front is that "release in a week" holds only when several conditions line up. Below, I break the waiting into pieces so you can work out your own date. Every figure here was checked against the official help pages in November 2026.

## Five kinds of waiting

Saying "review takes a while" hides where the days actually go. I count five separate waits. I used to count four — it took me a while to notice that Google Play's production access application is its own wait, with its own review.

Guidance is only guidance; it moves with the season and with your account's history. On the day you plan, I'd suggest opening Apple's [App Review](https://developer.apple.com/distribute/app-review/) page and Google's [testing requirements for new personal developer accounts](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465) once, just to see whether anything has changed.

## The 14 days people miss

If you're building as an individual, this is where I think estimates go wrong most often. Google's wording is precise: when you apply for production access, at least 12 testers must have been opted in to your closed test continuously for the preceding 14 days.

Reading that sentence slowly, I noticed two things.

First, **the 14 days effectively start when your twelfth tester joins.** Early testers may be well past two weeks, but if the twelfth signs up on day three of the test, you can't apply until day 17. So the estimate shouldn't be "14 days." It should include:

- the days it takes to gather testers (the most variable part)
- the 14 consecutive days after the twelfth one joins
- some slack for someone dropping out

Second, **a tester who leaves and comes back starts over.** Google's FAQ says that if someone opts out and later opts back in, their 14 days must be consecutive to count. A tester who switches phones or swaps Google accounts can quietly push your date back.

How fast people sign up is not something I control. I write that step as a range — three days to three weeks — and I invite around fifteen people rather than exactly twelve.

## After the 14 days, there's one more wait

Finishing the 14 days doesn't unlock the publish button. You apply for production access from the Play Console dashboard, and Google reviews the application. The help page says this usually takes seven days or less, and occasionally longer.

The form has three parts:

- About your closed test: how easy recruiting was, whether testers used every feature, what feedback you got and how you collected it
- About your app or game: the target audience, the value it offers, and an estimated install range for the first year
- About your production readiness: what you changed based on testing, and how you decided the app was ready

What caught me the first time was that Google asks for a *summary* of tester feedback. If you haven't been collecting any, there's nothing to summarize. Twelve people keeping an app installed for two weeks, without anyone saying anything, makes for a hollow fortnight whether or not the application passes.

Google also says it may ask you to keep testing if you have fewer than 12 opted-in testers or if engagement was too thin. So on the day the test starts, I decide where feedback goes — an email address works, and so does the Testing feedback page in Play Console.

## A script that works the date backward

Adding these up by hand each time is how I ended up frozen at the date field. The script below stacks the minimum and maximum of each wait, in order, and lays out both paths, iOS and Android. When the late-case path runs into the year-end window where Apple says reviews may take a bit longer, it adds a little slack, once.

```ts
// release-eta.ts
// Purpose: turn wait ranges into an earliest date and a late-case date, one path for iOS and one for Android
// Run: node --experimental-strip-types release-eta.ts (Node 22.6+) or npx tsx release-eta.ts
// The numbers mix official guidance with my own assumptions. None are guarantees, so check and overwrite them on the day you plan.

type Wait = { label: string; min: number; max: number; appleReview?: boolean }; // calendar days

const DAY = 86_400_000;
const parse = (s: string) => new Date(`${s}T00:00:00Z`); // read as UTC, print as UTC, so dates never shift
const addDays = (d: Date, n: number) => new Date(d.getTime() + n * DAY);
const fmt = (d: Date) => d.toISOString().slice(0, 10);

// The year-end window when Apple says reviews may take a bit longer. Check each December's notice and update it.
const SLOW = { from: parse("2026-12-23"), to: parse("2026-12-27"), extra: 3 };

function eta(startStr: string, steps: Wait[]) {
  let early = parse(startStr);
  let late = parse(startStr);
  let slowUsed = false; // add the year-end slack only once per path
  for (const s of steps) {
    const nextLate = addDays(late, s.max);
    // Only when the late-case path crosses the year-end window, add slack to Apple's review once
    const hit = !slowUsed && s.appleReview && late <= SLOW.to && nextLate >= SLOW.from;
    if (hit) slowUsed = true;
    early = addDays(early, s.min);
    late = hit ? addDays(nextLate, SLOW.extra) : nextLate;
    console.log(`  ${s.label}: ${fmt(early)} – ${fmt(late)}${hit ? " (year-end slack added)" : ""}`);
  }
  return { early, late };
}

const iosSteps: Wait[] = [
  { label: "Apple Developer enrollment", min: 1, max: 7 },
  { label: "First App Store review", min: 1, max: 2, appleReview: true }, // official: 90% within 48 hours
  { label: "One rejection and resubmission", min: 0, max: 3, appleReview: true },
];

const androidSteps: Wait[] = [
  { label: "Gather 12 testers", min: 3, max: 21 },
  { label: "14 consecutive closed-test days", min: 14, max: 14 },
  { label: "Production access review", min: 1, max: 7 }, // official: usually seven days or less
  { label: "First publishing review", min: 1, max: 7 },  // official: up to seven days or longer for some accounts
  { label: "One rejection and resubmission", min: 0, max: 3 },
];

for (const start of ["2026-11-24", "2026-12-14"]) {
  console.log(`\nStart ${start}`);
  console.log("[iOS]");
  const ios = eta(start, iosSteps);
  console.log("[Android]");
  const android = eta(start, androidSteps);
  // If you ship both, the slower path decides the date
  const slower = ios.late > android.late ? "iOS" : "Android";
  const both = new Date(Math.max(ios.late.getTime(), android.late.getTime()));
  console.log(`Both ready (late case): ${fmt(both)}  <- set by the ${slower} path`);
}
```

Starting on November 24, iOS lands in early December even in the late case, while Android crosses into the new year: 19 days at the earliest, 52 days if things run late. If you were planning to write "both stores before the holidays," this may be where your hand stops too.

The numbers matter less than seeing at a glance **which path is setting the date.** Before you write "in a week," it's worth running this once.

One correction to my own first version: it carried a comment saying the printed date "may be off by a day" because of UTC. It isn't. A date-only string like `"2026-11-24"` is parsed as midnight UTC, and `toISOString()` prints UTC, so the two agree. The shift only appears when you build a date in local time — `new Date(2026, 10, 24)` — and print it as UTC. Keeping both input and output in UTC removes the question.

When the estimate slips, I try not to conclude "review is slow" until I know which step is holding things up. These are the stalls I've run into, arranged so you can look them up by symptom.

The top three rows are all on the Android path. In my experience too, most of the delay happened before review, not during it.

## Personal and organization accounts wait differently

You might wonder whether an organization account is the way around the 14 days. The testing requirement does apply only to **personal** accounts created after November 13, 2023. But organizations have waits of their own.

</thead>
<tbody>

My rule of thumb is a single sentence: **if you already have a company and want the store to show its name, use an organization account; otherwise use a personal account and put the 14 days into your estimate.** Setting up a company just to skip the 14 days tends to trade one kind of waiting for another.

## Three common misreadings

**"Fourteen days of internal testing is enough."** It isn't. The requirement is a *closed* test. Internal testing reaches testers within seconds and is useful, but it doesn't count toward the 14 days. Check things in internal testing, then start the closed test early, and you won't lose days.

**"Apple reviews everything in 24 hours."** Half right. Apple's guidance is at least 50% within 24 hours and 90% within 48. A first submission may also bounce once. I budget two days for review and three for one rejection, separately.

**"The day the 14 days end is launch day."** After that come the production access review and the first publishing review. Give each about a week and you can be up to two weeks past day 14. The date in your announcement belongs at the end of all that.

## Budget three days for a rejection, because one may come

On a first review, it's sensible to assume it might not pass the first time. The three days in the script are for fixing, resubmitting, and waiting again.

The reason is often not the app itself but the paperwork around it: the description, the privacy details, or a missing demo login for the reviewer. Google's help page says the same thing — if your app needs sign-in, give reviewers working credentials in Play Console. So before I submit, I check once whether a reviewer could open everything without getting stuck.

I spend half a day on that check. Losing half a day is far cheaper than losing three.

## Wording the promise so it weighs less

A longer estimate isn't a reason to feel let down. Changing how you say it makes the promise much lighter.

- Write a range — "rolling out from the second week of the month" — rather than a single day.
- Give iOS and Android separate dates, and don't insist on a simultaneous launch.
- Pull forward the work that's yours alone, such as screenshots and store copy, into the waiting time.

If you'd like to pick the release moment yourself, I wrote about that separately in [You Cannot Shorten Review, But You Can Still Choose the Moment Your App Goes Live](/articles/rork-basics/rork-release-date-outside-the-review-queue). This piece is about knowing *when you can* ship; that one is about deciding *when you will*.

## Put the promised date after their waiting, not just your work

Since the night I stalled at that date field, I've changed one rule.

**A promised date goes after the other side's waiting, not just after my own work.**

Now I pick announcement dates from the late-case column. When things go faster, the only consequence is a pleasant surprise.

As tomorrow's first task, try changing the start date in the script to your own, filling in the days you've confirmed in the official help pages, and running it once. How far is the date it prints from the "one week" you had in mind? Knowing that gap is a good place to start.
