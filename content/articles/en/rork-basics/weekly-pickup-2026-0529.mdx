This is the Friday weekly pickup. We added about 36 articles between May 23 and May 29, and the ones that stayed with readers the longest were "implementation logs from operating six apps in parallel" and "production gotchas solved at the design layer." Rork Max is past feature-comparison territory; the phase now is operational sustainability.

I'm Masaki Hirokawa — artist and indie developer. I've been running my own app business since 2014 (50 million cumulative downloads, 17 international art awards), and I currently run six wallpaper apps in parallel on Rork Max while also operating four sites — Claude Lab, Gemini Lab, Antigravity Lab, and Rork Lab. All five picks below came directly out of that six-app daily verification.

## #1: Supporting iPhone Air and 17 Pro Max resolutions across six apps in parallel

[Supporting iPhone Air and 17 Pro Max resolutions across six apps in parallel](/articles/app-dev/iphone-air-17-pro-max-new-resolution-six-apps-parallel-support)

The week's top read was a log of fitting six wallpaper apps to Apple's fall resolutions at the same time. Every new device launch forces my apps to redo preview generation, SafeArea adjustments, and screenshots — across six binaries at once. Done by hand, that takes a week.

The article walks through defining resolution-aware layouts in Rork Max Screen Variants, sharing a common `wallpaperPresets.ts` across the six repos, and compressing the work into half a day. Useful reading if you're trying to shorten new-device support lead time.

## #2: Two months of Hermes in production — actual cold-start and memory numbers

[Two months of Hermes in production — actual cold-start and memory numbers](/articles/rork-dev/rork-hermes-two-months-production-cold-start-memory-review)

There's plenty of talk about Hermes being stable now, but very few numbers from running it across six apps for two months. The article publishes how much cold start improved vs JSC, how memory peaks moved, and which libraries broke under Hermes.

In my case, cold start on low-end devices dropped by about 31%. One charting library didn't render under Hermes, and I've documented the workaround.

## #3: Three weeks of migrating six wallpaper apps off MMKV to AsyncStorage

[Three weeks of migrating six wallpaper apps off MMKV to AsyncStorage](/articles/rork-dev/rork-mmkv-asyncstorage-migration-six-wallpaper-apps-3weeks)

`react-native-mmkv` is fast, but for two of my six apps, a specific React Native upgrade started producing startup crashes. The article is a three-week decision log for whether to keep MMKV or move back to AsyncStorage — and why a single answer across six apps was the wrong frame.

"Library speed" alone isn't the criterion; running different storage policies across different apps is acceptable. That's the conclusion after three weeks of actual operation.

## #4: Aggregating metrics across six apps daily on Cloudflare Workers

[Aggregating metrics across six apps daily on Cloudflare Workers](/articles/rork-dev/rork-multi-app-metrics-aggregation-cloudflare-workers-daily-digest)

Checking DAU, ARPDAU, crash-free rate, and AdMob revenue in six separate consoles every morning is no longer realistic, so I built a Cloudflare Workers pipeline. The article publishes the architecture: pull from App Store Connect API, Google Play Developer API, AdMob API, and Firebase Performance API, store in D1, and Slack-post at 8 AM. Workers TypeScript code included.

## #5: A three-layer sync design for StoreKit 2 subscription state

[A three-layer sync design for StoreKit 2 subscription state](/articles/rork-dev/rork-storekit-2-subscription-status-three-layer-sync)

Once you ship subscriptions, the most fragile failure is "the user paid, but the UI doesn't reflect it." Even StoreKit 2 leaves edge cases if you rely on `Transaction.updates` alone.

The article documents a three-layer sync — (1) the in-app `Transaction.updates`, (2) the server-side App Store Server API, (3) Server-Server Notifications v2 — with two months of operations logs from my wallpaper apps' premium tier.
