Earlier this year, a notice in Firebase's official docs caught my attention: new versions of the Firebase Apple SDK would stop being published to CocoaPods in October 2026. That is now what happened: since Firebase 13.0.0 on October 7, the SDK ships only through Swift Package Manager and binary distributions. I am revisiting this article in late November, about a week before CocoaPods trunk stops accepting new podspecs for good on December 2.

I maintain 4 iOS apps — Beautiful HD Wallpapers, Ukiyo-e Wallpapers, Relaxing Healing, and Law of Attraction Everyday. Every one of them ships Firebase Crashlytics and Analytics, which means every one of them is in scope for this change.

My first reaction was "October is months away." But once I actually started the migration, I kept hitting problems I hadn't anticipated — a Dropbox sync conflict that corrupted SPM's index, a dSYM upload script pointing at a path that no longer existed, and an issue specific to Rork Max where project regeneration silently restored the Podfile I had just deleted.

I started with **Relaxing Healing** as a pilot, and documenting what broke made the remaining three apps dramatically faster. This article is the full log.

## Why This Is Worth Doing Now

CocoaPods was the dominant iOS package manager for most of the 2010s. Apple integrating Swift Package Manager into Xcode natively changed the trajectory, and Firebase's October 2026 cutoff is the clearest possible signal about where things are heading.

Checking the migration guide and release notes again in November 2026, this is the actual sequence — narrower than the headlines suggest:

<table>
<tr><th>Date (2026)</th><th>What happened</th><th>What it means if you're on CocoaPods</th></tr>
<tr><td>Sep 9</td><td>Firebase 12.19.0: the final planned 12.x minor and the final scheduled release published to CocoaPods</td><td>New features stop reaching a Podfile from here</td></tr>
<tr><td>Oct 7</td><td>Firebase 13.0.0: Swift Package Manager and binary distributions only</td><td><code>pod update</code> will never pull 13</td></tr>
<tr><td>Oct 22</td><td>Firebase 12.19.3: a Cloud Firestore security update for Firebase 12, explicitly including CocoaPods projects</td><td>Critical 12.x fixes can still appear, but none are scheduled</td></tr>
<tr><td>Dec 2</td><td>CocoaPods trunk permanently stops accepting new podspecs (after a Nov 2–6 test run)</td><td>Existing versions stay installable</td></tr>
</table>

So Firebase 12 is the last major on CocoaPods, versions 12 and earlier remain installable, and Firebase's own FAQ says your app won't stop working if you don't update. Nothing broke overnight. But every fix and feature in 13 and later is unreachable from a Podfile, and over time that gap shows up as slower support for new iOS versions and App Store requirements.

For an independent developer running real apps with real users, this is concrete risk — not hypothetical. I've watched a dependency fall behind before: crashes start appearing on new iOS versions, review slows down while Apple flags outdated dependencies, and recovery ends up far more disruptive than a planned migration would have been.

Beyond Firebase, the CocoaPods maintainers have announced the trunk read-only plan step by step on their own blog. Existing builds keep working by design, but after December 2 no dependency will be updated through trunk again. Migrating on your own schedule beats doing it under pressure.

## Work Backwards From the Cutoff: Move Now, or Wait on v12

My first instinct was "October is months away." Looking back, the real mistake was not the date itself — it was never checking, against the primary source, what actually stops after the date.

**Decide the order by what stops after the deadline, not by the deadline itself** — that is the rule I use now.

<table>
<tr><th>Your situation</th><th>Choice</th><th>Why</th></tr>
<tr><td>You need new Firebase features or fixes</td><td>Migrate to SPM now</td><td>Nothing from 13.0.0 (Oct 7) onward is on CocoaPods</td></tr>
<tr><td>Your current version is enough for now</td><td>Pin v12 or earlier and stay on CocoaPods, if you must</td><td>Existing v12-and-earlier releases stay installable, but CocoaPods trunk stops accepting podspecs on December 2, 2026</td></tr>
<tr><td>You can't update Xcode yet</td><td>Check the Xcode requirement before migrating</td><td>Firebase 13's Swift package will not resolve in Xcode versions earlier than 26.2 (release notes, as of November 2026)</td></tr>
<tr><td>You still support iOS 14</td><td>Move to SPM pinned to 12.x; go to 13 only after deciding your minimum OS</td><td>Firebase 13's minimum is iOS 15.0</td></tr>
<tr><td>CI runs <code>pod install</code></td><td>Switch CI to SPM resolution together with the app</td><td>Without a committed Package.resolved, CI and your Mac drift apart</td></tr>

I spell out the "you can wait" option to be fair, not to slow you down: lay out the reasons to hurry and the reasons to wait, then choose for your own apps.

- **Relaxing Healing**: `Firebase/Core`, `Firebase/Crashlytics`, `Firebase/Analytics`, `Firebase/Performance`
- **Law of Attraction Everyday**: `Firebase/Core`, `Firebase/Crashlytics`, `Firebase/Analytics`, `Firebase/RemoteConfig`

All four projects use Rork Max for development and maintenance work, but Firebase configuration is handled directly in Xcode. There's a specific Rork Max concern to be aware of around project regeneration, which I'll cover later.

## Pre-Migration: Auditing Your CocoaPods Dependencies

Before removing anything, list every pod in your Podfile and verify SPM availability for each one.

```ruby
# Relaxing Healing Podfile (before migration)
platform :ios, '14.0'
use_frameworks!

target 'RelaxingHealing' do
  pod 'Firebase/Core'
  pod 'Firebase/Crashlytics'
  pod 'Firebase/Analytics'
  pod 'Firebase/Performance'
end
```

The SPM equivalents map directly:

- `Firebase/Core` → `FirebaseCore`
- `Firebase/Crashlytics` → `FirebaseCrashlytics`
- `Firebase/Analytics` → `FirebaseAnalytics` (or `FirebaseAnalyticsWithoutAdIdSupport`)
- `Firebase/Performance` → `FirebasePerformance`

One decision to make on Analytics: the standard `FirebaseAnalytics` includes IDFA access. If your app doesn't request ATT authorization, `FirebaseAnalyticsWithoutAdIdSupport` avoids review friction. My apps all use ATT prompts, so I stayed with the standard variant.

If you have non-Firebase pods that don't have SPM support, you won't be able to remove Podfile entirely. An earlier version of this article said you could run Firebase through SPM while keeping other dependencies in CocoaPods. I need to correct that: Firebase's migration guide says mixing CocoaPods and SPM within the same target can lead to dependency cycles and build errors, and recommends manual installation (the XCFrameworks in `Firebase.zip`) as the stable path while your other dependencies catch up. For all four of my apps, Firebase was the only CocoaPods dependency, so I could do a clean removal.

## Record Your Resolved Versions Before Deleting `Podfile.lock`

One ordering decision paid off more than anything else here: **pin the Firebase version in SPM to whatever is currently building, before you change anything else.**

If you delete `Podfile.lock` along with the rest of CocoaPods and then pick "Up to Next Major Version" in Xcode's Package Dependencies, you have just done two migrations at once — CocoaPods to SPM, and one Firebase version to another. When something breaks after that, you can't tell whether it's the package manager or the SDK. That's exactly what happened on Relaxing Healing, and it cost me roughly an extra hour chasing a linker error that turned out to be unrelated to SPM.

Capture the resolved versions first:

```bash
# Extract only the resolved Firebase versions from Podfile.lock
grep -E '^[[:space:]]+- Firebase' Podfile.lock | sed 's/^[[:space:]]*//'

# Example output
#   - Firebase/Analytics (11.8.0)
#   - Firebase/Core (11.8.0)
#   - Firebase/Crashlytics (11.8.0)
#   - Firebase/Performance (11.8.0)
```

Enter that version in Xcode as an **Exact Version**, and split the migration into two commits so each class of failure has an unambiguous cause:

<table>
<tr><th>Stage</th><th>What you do</th><th>What a failure here means</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Commit 1</strong></td><td>Add Firebase via SPM pinned to the exact version from <code>Podfile.lock</code></td><td>Build errors, linker errors, missing dSYM uploads are <strong>SPM configuration</strong> problems</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Commit 2</strong></td><td>Switch to Up to Next Major Version and update to current</td><td>Launch crashes or changed analytics behavior are <strong>Firebase version</strong> problems</td></tr>
</table>

Once commit 1 builds, Xcode generates `Package.resolved`. Commit that file. It plays the same role `Podfile.lock` did — without it under version control, a fresh clone on another machine can silently resolve to different versions. Its location depends on your project structure, so search for it rather than guessing:

```bash
find . -name "Package.resolved" -not -path "*/DerivedData/*"
# .../MyApp.xcodeproj/project.xcworkspace/xcshareddata/swiftpm/Package.resolved
```

## Create a Restore Point Before Running `pod deintegrate`

`pod deintegrate` is not a command that deletes your Podfile. It rewrites the inside of `.xcodeproj` — build phases, xcconfig references, framework linkage. If you decide midway that you want to go back, reconstructing that by hand is not realistic.

I assumed Dropbox version history had me covered. It didn't. An `.xcodeproj` is a bundle of files, and Dropbox restores one file at a time — it's the wrong tool for rolling a project back to a consistent state.

Make a git restore point first:

```bash
# Commit and tag before touching anything
git add -A
git commit -m "chore: snapshot before SPM migration"
git tag pre-spm-relaxing-healing

# If you need to go back (restores Podfile, Podfile.lock, and .xcodeproj together)
git checkout pre-spm-relaxing-healing -- .
pod install
```

While you're there, stop tracking the working files SPM generates. Skipping this is how fragments of the Dropbox conflict copies described below end up committed:

```
# Add to .gitignore
.swiftpm/
DerivedData/
*.xcworkspace/xcuserdata/
*.xcodeproj/xcuserdata/
```

The app name in the tag matters more than it looks. Working through four apps in sequence, a bare `pre-spm` tag stops telling you which project it belongs to by the second one.

## Step-by-Step Migration — The Relaxing Healing Walkthrough

### Step 1: Remove CocoaPods Completely with pod deintegrate

Don't just delete the Podfile — run `pod deintegrate` first to cleanly remove all CocoaPods integration artifacts from your `.xcodeproj`.

```bash
cd /path/to/RelaxingHealing
pod deintegrate

# Remove Podfile and related files
rm -f Podfile Podfile.lock
rm -rf Pods/
```

After this, use `.xcodeproj` directly in Xcode. The `.xcworkspace` file was generated by CocoaPods and is no longer needed once Pods are gone.

Firebase's official order is to run `pod deintegrate` **with Xcode closed**, delete the CocoaPods-generated `.xcworkspace`, then add packages via **File → Add Package Dependencies**.

### Step 2: Add Firebase via Swift Package Dependencies

In Xcode, with your `.xcodeproj` open:

1. Select the project in the navigator
2. Open the **Package Dependencies** tab
3. Click **+** to add a package
4. Enter `https://github.com/firebase/firebase-ios-sdk`
5. Choose a version (latest stable recommended)
6. Select only the modules you actually use

```
✅ FirebaseCore
✅ FirebaseCrashlytics
✅ FirebaseAnalytics
✅ FirebasePerformance
```

Only check what you were using via CocoaPods. Adding modules you don't need increases binary size and build time. The first package resolution downloads the full SDK and takes a few minutes. Subsequent builds use cached packages and are significantly faster.

If the build then complains that a Firebase library can't be found, the module usually isn't linked to the target. Firebase's FAQ points to **Build Phases → Link Binary with Libraries**; apps with extra targets (widgets, notification extensions) are where I'd look first.

### Step 3: Remove CocoaPods Residue from Build Settings

Even after `pod deintegrate`, some CocoaPods-injected settings can linger. Open Build Settings in Xcode and search for `PODS`. Remove any remaining references.

```
Things to check and remove:
  Header Search Paths: $(PODS_ROOT)/... entries
  Library Search Paths: $(PODS_ROOT)/... entries
```

One exception: don't delete `-ObjC` mechanically. Firebase's installation docs say Analytics needs the `-ObjC` linker flag when it is pulled in transitively. An earlier version of this article called it unnecessary under SPM; that was wrong. I now leave it in place and only tidy it up after the build and a device check both pass.

Build the project and confirm it compiles cleanly before moving on.

## Pitfall 1: Dropbox Conflict Copies Corrupting SPM Index

This was the most time-consuming problem I ran into, and it's completely undocumented.

I sync my projects through Dropbox across multiple Macs. After adding Firebase via SPM, Xcode creates `.swiftpm/xcuserdata` directories inside the project. Dropbox aggressively syncs these, and when Xcode was open on two machines simultaneously, the file locking conflicts corrupted SPM's package index.

The symptom: `error: cannot find module 'Firebase' in scope` appeared in the editor persistently, even though clean builds succeeded. Autocomplete stopped working. The inconsistency between editor behavior and build results is the signature of this problem.

The fix is to mark the affected directories as Dropbox-ignored using `xattr` — the same attribute Dropbox's Help Center documents for ignoring files and folders on macOS:

```bash
# Run from your project root
xattr -w com.dropbox.ignored 1 ".swiftpm"
xattr -w com.dropbox.ignored 1 "$(find . -name 'xcshareddata' -path '*swiftpm*' 2>/dev/null | head -1)"
```

After applying this, quit Xcode, let Dropbox finish syncing, then reopen the project. The module errors cleared up immediately and have not recurred.

I had run into similar Dropbox sync issues with CocoaPods' `xcuserdata` directories before. SPM's paths are different, but the underlying problem is the same. When an unexplained build error shows up after migration, this is the first thing I run:

```bash
find . \( -name '*conflicted copy*' -o -name '*競合しているコピー*' \) -not -path '*/DerivedData/*'
```
 If you manage iOS projects in Dropbox, apply this fix before opening the project in Xcode for the first time after migration.

## Pitfall 2: The dSYM Upload Script That Breaks Silently

Firebase Crashlytics requires a Run Script build phase to upload dSYM files for crash symbolication. The CocoaPods version references `$(PODS_ROOT)`:

```bash
# Old CocoaPods version — breaks after migration
"${PODS_ROOT}/FirebaseCrashlytics/run"
```

After removing CocoaPods, this path doesn't exist. Depending on how your Run Script phase is configured, this either causes a build error or fails silently — the latter being worse, because builds succeed but your crash reports in App Store Connect will show unsymbolicated hex addresses instead of file names and line numbers. You won't notice the problem until you're looking at an actual user crash.

The SPM replacement path:

```bash
# Swift Package Manager version
"${BUILD_DIR%/Build/*}/SourcePackages/checkouts/firebase-ios-sdk/Crashlytics/run"
```

For a more robust version that's resilient to DerivedData location changes:

```bash
#!/bin/bash
# Firebase Crashlytics dSYM upload — SPM version

FIREBASE_RUN=$(find "${BUILD_DIR%/Build/*}/SourcePackages/checkouts" \
  -name "run" \
  -path "*/Crashlytics/run" \
  2>/dev/null | head -1)

if [ -z "$FIREBASE_RUN" ]; then
  echo "error: Firebase Crashlytics run script not found."
  exit 1
fi

"$FIREBASE_RUN"
```

Failing loudly matters here. An earlier version of this script ended with `exit 0` when the path wasn't found, which quietly recreates the exact "build succeeds, dSYMs never arrive" problem described above.

Two more details from Firebase's official steps: this script must be the project's **last** build phase, and its Input Files should list the dSYM and app binary paths:

```
${DWARF_DSYM_FOLDER_PATH}/${DWARF_DSYM_FILE_NAME}
${DWARF_DSYM_FOLDER_PATH}/${DWARF_DSYM_FILE_NAME}/Contents/Resources/DWARF/${PRODUCT_NAME}
${DWARF_DSYM_FOLDER_PATH}/${DWARF_DSYM_FILE_NAME}/Contents/Info.plist
$(TARGET_BUILD_DIR)/$(UNLOCALIZED_RESOURCES_FOLDER_PATH)/GoogleService-Info.plist
$(TARGET_BUILD_DIR)/$(EXECUTABLE_PATH)
```

If both `ENABLE_USER_SCRIPT_SANDBOXING` and `ENABLE_DEBUG_DYLIB` are on, add the `.../DWARF/${PRODUCT_NAME}.debug.dylib` path as well. Missing inputs let the script sandbox block file reads, which is another way for dSYMs to go missing without a visible error.

Also check this setting, which is easy to overlook (the official steps set it for all build types):

```
Build Settings → Debug Information Format
Release: DWARF with dSYM File   ← Required for symbolication to work
(NOT just "DWARF" — that omits dSYM output entirely)
```

## Pitfall 3: Editor Module Errors That Persist After Successful Builds

A common post-migration symptom: the app builds and runs correctly, but Xcode's editor shows persistent red underlines on Firebase imports with `module 'FirebaseCore' not found`. Autocomplete is broken. Everything looks wrong but actually works.

This is a stale Xcode index cache. The fix:

```bash
# Step 1: Delete Derived Data for your app
rm -rf ~/Library/Developer/Xcode/DerivedData/<AppName>-*

# Then: restart Xcode

# Step 2: Product → Clean Build Folder (Cmd+Shift+K)

# Step 3: File → Packages → Reset Package Caches
```

This three-step sequence resolves it almost every time. If the errors persist, close the project completely and reopen the `.xcodeproj`. I've seen this issue occur once or twice after the initial migration; after that, it doesn't come back.

## Build Performance After Migration

The first full build after adding Firebase via SPM is noticeably slower — SPM compiles and caches packages fresh. This is expected. The issue is if every subsequent build is also slow.

If incremental builds are slower than before, check your Run Script build phases. Any script running unconditionally adds time on every build. Enable "Based on dependency analysis" on scripts that don't need to run when nothing has changed:

```
Build Phases → Each Run Script phase
→ ✅ Check "Based on dependency analysis"
→ The Firebase Crashlytics dSYM script will only run when the binary changes
```

Enabling this on Relaxing Healing brought incremental build times from about 6 minutes back down to around 90 seconds. The difference matters significantly during active development when you're building frequently.

## Expanding to the Other Three Apps

With Relaxing Healing done, I migrated Beautiful HD Wallpapers, Ukiyo-e Wallpapers, and Law of Attraction Everyday using a consistent pre-migration script:

```bash
#!/bin/bash
# pre-spm-migration.sh — run before opening each project in Xcode

echo "1. Deintegrating CocoaPods..."
pod deintegrate

echo "2. Removing CocoaPods files..."
rm -f Podfile Podfile.lock
rm -rf Pods/

echo "3. Setting Dropbox ignore attributes..."
xattr -w com.dropbox.ignored 1 ".swiftpm" 2>/dev/null || true
find . -name "xcshareddata" -path "*swiftpm*" \
  -exec xattr -w com.dropbox.ignored 1 {} \; 2>/dev/null || true

echo "4. Backing up current dSYM script path..."
grep -r "PODS_ROOT.*Crashlytics" . --include="*.pbxproj" \
  > dSYM_backup.txt 2>/dev/null || true

echo "Done. Open .xcodeproj in Xcode and add Firebase via Package Dependencies."
```

Running this script first meant the Dropbox conflict issue and the dSYM path problem were both pre-empted before I even opened Xcode. The Relaxing Healing migration took 4 hours of debugging; each of the other three took about 30 minutes.

## Coexisting With AppLovin MAX Mediation

Beautiful HD Wallpapers uses AppLovin MAX for AdMob mediation, and its iOS SDK was also managed through CocoaPods. That raised an obvious question: migrate both at once, or one at a time?

AppLovin MAX itself supports SPM. The variable is the mediation adapters — each partner adapter is a separate package, and SPM support arrived at different times for each. As of May 2026, the adapters I depend on were all available:

```
AppLovin MAX SPM readiness (checked 2026-05):
✅ AppLovin MAX SDK core: SPM supported
✅ Liftoff adapter: SPM supported
✅ InMobi adapter: SPM supported
✅ Unity Ads adapter: SPM supported
⚠️ Smaller / regional adapters: verify individually
```

Check each adapter against its partner's own documentation rather than assuming parity with the core SDK — that's where a mediation stack tends to break.

The migration steps are identical to Firebase. An earlier version of this article said AppLovin MAX needs its own `AppLovinSettings.plist`. AppLovin's current iOS integration guide (checked November 2026) doesn't mention that file; the SDK key is passed in code through `ALSdkInitializationConfiguration`. After migrating, the first thing I check is that the initialization code still compiles against the SPM imports.

I migrated Firebase first and left AppLovin MAX on CocoaPods for two weeks. Keeping the ad stack unchanged meant that if revenue moved, I knew it wasn't the mediation layer. Given Firebase's guidance against mixing CocoaPods and SPM in one target, though, if I made the same call today I'd keep that window as short as I could.

## Managing Firebase Versions After Migration

One practical benefit of SPM over CocoaPods: version updates happen entirely within Xcode's UI. No terminal commands, no Podfile edits. From the Package Dependencies tab, you can see the current version of each package and update with a click.

For major version updates, Firebase occasionally introduces breaking changes. My practice is to test a new major version on one app before rolling it out to all four. Relaxing Healing serves as the pilot — if it runs cleanly for a week in TestFlight, I apply the same version to the others.

On version strategy: "Up to Next Major Version" is the default, which means Xcode will automatically pull minor and patch updates. For apps running stably in production, I prefer "Exact Version" for major packages so updates happen deliberately rather than automatically. I've had minor Firebase updates change logging behavior unexpectedly in the past, and controlling when updates happen makes it easier to isolate the cause of any new issues.

The Firebase SDK release notes are published on GitHub and in the Firebase blog. Major releases typically include a migration guide. Reading the release notes before updating has saved me debugging time more than once.

## Rork Max Specific: Podfile Restoration After Project Regeneration

If you use Rork Max to manage your project, be aware that **project regeneration can restore the Podfile you deleted**.

Rork Max generates iOS project structure based on your configuration, and depending on your settings, it may output a Podfile as part of that structure. After SPM migration, if you regenerate the project with Rork Max for any reason, you might find a fresh Podfile has silently appeared.

Two ways to handle this:

**Option A**: Look for a Rork Max setting that stops CocoaPods output. I couldn't confirm from Rork's official material, as of November 2026, that such a setting exists — if you can't find it, rely on Option B.

**Option B**: Add a post-generation validation step to your workflow:

```bash
#!/bin/bash
# post-rork-generate.sh — run after any Rork Max project regeneration

if [ -f "Podfile" ]; then
  echo "⚠️  Podfile detected — possibly restored by Rork Max regeneration"
  echo "🗑  Removing to preserve SPM migration..."
  rm -f Podfile Podfile.lock
  rm -rf Pods/
  echo "✅  Removed. Verify Package Dependencies in Xcode."
fi
```

This script takes a couple of seconds and prevents silent regressions that are easy to miss in the middle of an active development session.

## Post-Migration Verification Checklist

Before submitting to App Store, confirm:

```
□ App launches cleanly on physical device
□ Firebase Analytics events appear in Firebase Console (within ~1 hour)
□ Test crash (e.g. fatalError("Crash was triggered")) registers in Crashlytics dashboard
□ Crash report shows symbolicated stack trace with file names and line numbers
□ Release Archive → Distribute App completes without errors
□ TestFlight build installs and runs on device
□ No $(PODS_ROOT) references remain in Build Settings
□ No Podfile present in project root
```

The symbolication check is the one most commonly skipped. You need to explicitly confirm that a crash report shows file names and line numbers — not just hex addresses. The only way to know dSYM upload is working correctly is to trigger a test crash and verify what appears in the Crashlytics dashboard.

A correction to my earlier checklist: `Crashlytics.crashlytics().crash()` is not an API in Firebase's current SDK. The official sample simply calls `fatalError("Crash was triggered")`, and you should run it with the Xcode debugger detached (stop the run, then relaunch the app from the device) — with the debugger attached, the crash report is never sent.

The first three rows all split on one question: does the build itself fail, or only the editor? That's always where I start.

## Three Common Misreadings, Checked Against the Official Wording

**"After October, CocoaPods apps stop working."** They don't. Firebase's FAQ says v12 and earlier remain installable and functional as long as CocoaPods ecosystem support continues, and CI running `pod install` keeps working. What stopped is new versions arriving.

**"Stay on CocoaPods and 12.x gets nothing ever again."** Not quite. 12.19.3 on October 22 was a Cloud Firestore security update that explicitly covered CocoaPods projects. But 12.19.0 was described as the final scheduled release, so I don't treat further 12.x fixes as something to wait for.

**"Move Firebase to SPM and leave everything else in CocoaPods."** Firebase's migration guide advises against mixing the two in the same target. If other dependencies lack SPM support, the documented route is manual installation of Firebase via `Firebase.zip`.

## The Next Thing You'll Hit: Before Commit 2 Takes You to Firebase 13

Once commit 1 is green on SPM, the next step is switching to Up to Next Major Version and moving to 13. Reading the 13.0.0–13.2.0 release notes in November 2026, these are the three things I'd check first:

- The minimum iOS version is now 15.0 (the Podfile at the top of this article said `platform :ios, '14.0'`)
- `FirebaseMLModelDownloader` has been removed
- At 13.0.0, a Google Sign-In release compatible with Firebase 13 via SPM was not yet available and was promised for the following weeks

If your app signs users in with Google, that third one is worth checking before commit 2, not after.

For how much crash collection to keep in Crashlytics, I've written up [starting with the EAS Observe free tier, and why Crashlytics stays installed](/en/articles/rork-basics/eas-observe-free-tier-native-crash-gap-first-setup). For which dependency to check first the night before submission, [The Night Before You Submit: Which Dependencies to Check First](/en/articles/rork-business/store-submission-dependency-check-order-for-non-engineers) is a reasonable starting order. And the habit of keeping `.swiftpm/` and `xcuserdata` out of version control comes from the same place as [writing .gitignore before you run git init on an exported Rork project](/en/articles/rork-basics/rork-export-gitignore-before-git-init).

## Start With One App, Pinned to 12.x

The deadline looks like it has passed, but the only thing that actually stopped was new versions arriving. Rather than jumping straight to 13, move to SPM on the version that builds today, then upgrade — that two-step order helped more than anything else across all four apps. Most of the four hours on Relaxing Healing went into the Dropbox conflict and the dSYM script path, not the migration itself.

If you're using Rork Max, the one extra habit is checking that no Podfile reappeared after a regeneration. Everything else maps directly to a standard Xcode project.

For the first week after migrating, I watch the Crashlytics dashboard more often than usual, because configuration mistakes tend to show up as missing reports rather than errors. Keep App Store updates on hold and let TestFlight carry that week.

Your next step: open the `Podfile.lock` of the app with the fewest Firebase modules and copy down its resolved versions. Even after December 2, those few lines keep a way back open.
