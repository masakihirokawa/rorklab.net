The day after last September's release, the tone in my review section shifted. Nobody wrote "it crashes." They wrote "the title overlaps the status bar" and "the bottom button is hard to tap."

Not a line of my code had changed. What changed was the phone in their hand.

When you run a handful of apps as an indie developer, the days right after a major iOS release are quietly unsettling. For the past few years I've kept one spare iPhone set aside as a test device, so I can walk through my own apps on the new OS. No Mac, no Xcode — just an old phone and half an hour.

The one thing I'd put up front: **on a new OS, the build worth checking is the one in the App Store, not the one on your laptop.** What follows is the routine I use — what to look at, how to tell causes apart, and how to ship the fix.

## Count the Gap That Remains After Release

Once a release ships, users' phones move to it within days. Your fix, on the other hand, has to be rewritten, rebuilt, and pass review before it arrives. **The user's environment always changes first; your response always trails behind.** The only way to shorten that gap is to look early.

During the beta there's one more constraint: an app built with a beta SDK can go to TestFlight but cannot be submitted for review. I wrote that up in [A beta-SDK build can reach TestFlight, but it can't reach review](/articles/rork-dev/rork-beta-sdk-build-testflight-ok-app-store-submission-blocked).

So of the three steps — check on the new OS, fix, ship — the last one stays locked during the beta. Now that the release is out, you can do all three. Even so, it's a very different load from hunting for causes after the reviews pile up. The years I opened the review section first were the years my stomach hurt for a week.

## Set Aside One Spare Device

Don't test on your main phone.

Going back from a beta requires erasing and restoring the device, and a backup made on a newer iOS can't be restored onto an older one. Put your main phone on a beta and you're effectively stuck there until the next release. That's not a risk worth carrying on the phone you use for calls and payments.

Preparing the spare takes three steps.

1. Sign in to the spare iPhone with your Apple ID and make a backup.
2. In Settings → General → Software Update, install the latest public release (the 27.2 line, as of November). When it's time to try the next beta, pick it from Beta Updates on the same screen.
3. Install your own live app from the App Store, the normal way.

Step 3 is the whole point. Not a development build, not a Rork Companion preview — **exactly the version your users have right now.**

I learned that the hard way. One year I checked a new OS against my development build, called it clean, and a week later a review said the bottom button couldn't be tapped. The dev build bundled slightly different assets and had slightly different settings enabled than the store build. I only realized that most "it works on my machine but the reviews say otherwise" cases come from this gap after I'd finished replying to that review.

The line I've drawn since then is a single sentence: **check what's in the store, fix what's on my desk.** I don't swap that order.

Any old phone from a drawer will do. No SIM needed — just Wi-Fi and an App Store sign-in. Do check Apple's compatibility list first to make sure the device can run iOS 27.

## Decide the Check Order by What Breaks Most

Once the device is ready, don't poke around at random. The table below is ordered so that wide-impact, cheap-to-fix areas come first.

<tbody>

In my experience the first three catch most of what you'll find. Further down, problems are rarer but more likely to sit unnoticed for months.

Row 4 is new this year. With iOS 27, Apple made it clear that app launch and resume should go through the scene-based (UIScene) life cycle. Your live build still runs on the SDK it was built with, so nothing necessarily breaks today. But that's exactly where an SDK upgrade tends to bite, so recording how it behaves now on the spare phone gives you a baseline to compare against later.

The real reason to fix the order in advance isn't time pressure — it's so you can **sweep every app with the same lens.** When you maintain several wallpaper apps, there's no room to inspect each one lovingly. "Check row 1 in every app, then row 2 in every app" keeps my judgment consistent.

## Tell the Cause Apart From the Symptom

When the spare phone turns something up, the first question is always: is it the OS, my code, or a dependency? Here is the table I actually use.

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Symptom</th><th>Likely causes</th><th>How to confirm</th><th>Fix</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Header or bottom button overlaps a system bar</td><td>Safe-area values hard-coded; SafeAreaView applied twice</td><td>Open the same screen on an older-iOS device side by side and compare the spacing</td><td>Read insets as numbers with useSafeAreaInsets (code below). <tr><td>Button label spills out of its frame</td><td>The frame doesn't keep up with Dynamic Type scaling</td><td>Set Accessibility → Larger Text to max and open the same screen</td><td>Cap maxFontSizeMultiplier only on fixed-height elements. Ships via EAS Update.</td></tr>
<tr><td>App doesn't return after picking a photo</td><td>The picker library meets a changed OS</td><td>Run npx expo install --check for version drift; try to reproduce in a blank app</td><td>Update the library. If it includes native code, rebuild and submit for review.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Permission dialog text is cut off oddly</td><td>Info.plist string too long, or a missing localization</td><td>Switch the device language and trigger the same dialog</td><td>Fix infoPlist in app.json and rebuild. Needs review.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Notification tap doesn't reach the target screen</td><td>Cold start and resume take different code paths</td><td>Tap the notification with the app fully closed and again with it in the background</td><td>EAS Update if it's JS navigation; SDK update and rebuild if it's the native entry point</td></tr>

The column that earns its keep is "How to confirm." Before blaming the new OS, putting an older device next to it and trying a blank app will show you that about half the time it's my own code.

## What You Can Fix in JS Right Now

There are three places you can work on without waiting for the device results. All three are one step beyond patterns that show up often in Rork-generated code.

### Read the Bottom Inset as a Number, Not Through SafeAreaView

```tsx
import { SafeAreaProvider, useSafeAreaInsets } from 'react-native-safe-area-context';
import { View, Pressable, Text, StyleSheet } from 'react-native';

function Footer() {
  const insets = useSafeAreaInsets();

  return (
    <View style={[styles.footer, { paddingBottom: insets.bottom + 12 }]}>
      <Pressable style={styles.button}>
        <Text style={styles.label}>Save</Text>
      </Pressable>
    </View>
  );
}

export default function App() {
  return (
    <SafeAreaProvider>
      <Footer />
    </SafeAreaProvider>
  );
}

const styles = StyleSheet.create({
  footer: { paddingHorizontal: 16, paddingTop: 12 },
  button: { minHeight: 44, alignItems: 'center', justifyContent: 'center' },
  label: { fontSize: 17 },
});
```

`SafeAreaView` applies the device's safe area directly as padding. It's convenient, but the moment you want a bit of your own spacing on top, you swing between double padding and not enough.

`useSafeAreaInsets` returns the same values as numbers, so you can write `insets.bottom + 12` and fold them into your own layout. If the OS changes how the home indicator is handled, only `insets.bottom` changes; your layout math survives. Whether you hold this as a number decides how much work each new iOS season costs me.

### Cap Text Scaling Only Where the Frame Is Fixed

```tsx
<Text
  style={{ fontSize: 15 }}
  maxFontSizeMultiplier={1.4}
  numberOfLines={1}
  ellipsizeMode="tail"
>
  Save settings
</Text>
```

I'd rather not reach for `allowFontScaling={false}` to stop scaling everywhere. That overrides the setting of someone who reads with larger text, for my convenience.

Instead, I cap the multiplier **only on elements with a fixed height**, like button and tab labels. Body text scales freely; only the places that become untappable when they break get protected.

### A Version Check Means Different Things on Each Platform

```tsx
import { Platform } from 'react-native';

// On iOS, Platform.Version is a string like "27.2"
// On Android, Platform.Version is a number: the API level (e.g. 36)
const iosMajor =
  Platform.OS === 'ios' ? parseInt(String(Platform.Version), 10) : 0;

export const isIOS27OrLater = iosMajor >= 27;
```

`Platform.Version` returns the OS version string on iOS and the API level number on Android. The meaning itself differs.

A bare `Platform.Version >= 27` means "iOS 27 or later" on iOS and "API level 27 or later" — Android 8.1 and up — on Android. Because it runs without error, it's easy to miss on a later read. Split on `Platform.OS` first and pin the meaning in a name, as above.

And there's no need to add branches the moment a new OS ships. Try it without branching first, and add one only where behavior actually changed. The branches I wrote ahead of time almost always turned out to be unnecessary.

## Three Misconceptions Worth Untangling

### "If it breaks on the new OS, upgrading the SDK will fix it"

Your live build runs on the new OS with the SDK it was built with. Most layout breakage comes from how the layout is written, and an SDK bump won't touch it. An SDK bump does, however, mean a native rebuild, a review, and extra changes on your side such as the scene life cycle migration. **Fixing breakage and upgrading the SDK are two separate jobs**, and keeping them apart keeps my decisions steady.

### "If it looks fine in Rork Companion or Expo Go, the store build is fine too"

Preview apps are a different binary from your store build. Native modules, permission strings, icons, and launch screens all differ. A preview shows you JavaScript behavior and nothing more; it can't stand in for checking the store build.

### "JS fixes reach every device through EAS Update"

An EAS update is delivered only to builds whose runtime version matches. If several versions of your app are still in users' hands and an older one has a different runtime version, those users never get the update. When you've shipped a fix and it still isn't showing up, I walk through the causes in [EAS Update Published but Nothing Changes? Five Patterns That Quietly Break OTA Delivery in Rork](/articles/rork-dev/rork-eas-update-ota-not-reflecting-troubleshooting).

## After You Find Something: What You Can Fix and What Has to Wait

When something turns up, check for dependency drift first.

```bash
npx expo install --check
npx expo-doctor
```

`expo install --check` compares your installed packages against the current SDK and suggests the right versions if they've drifted. `expo-doctor` goes further, checking config files and native-side mismatches. Steadying your footing with these two before suspecting the OS makes the rest of the diagnosis faster.

From there, fixes take one of two routes.

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Kind of fix</th><th>Examples</th><th>How it ships</th><th>Time to reach users</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>JS layer</td><td>Layout, text sizing, conditionals, copy, navigation logic</td><td>EAS Update</td><td>No review. Reaches builds with a matching runtime version on a later launch.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Native config</td><td>Permission strings, Info.plist, adding or updating native modules</td><td>Rebuild and submit for review</td><td>Days, because you wait for review and for users to update.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

Knowing where that line falls sets the order of work right after a release. Fix everything you can in JS and ship it, then bundle the native fixes into a single build for review. Submitting two or three times in a panic costs days each time.

## SDK 58: Now, or in the Spring?

Expo SDK 58 is out with iOS 27 in mind, bringing the scene-based launch flow and a newer React Native. I decide on the upgrade only after the urgent breakage is fixed.

The rule fits in one line: **if a native rebuild is coming anyway, upgrade along with it; if JS fixes are enough, wait for spring.** Once you decide to upgrade, running [A Script to Find the React Native APIs SDK 58 Removed, Before You Upgrade](/articles/app-dev/expo-sdk58-removed-react-native-apis-scan-script-before-upgrade) against your code first gives you a realistic view of the work.

## Where You're Likely to Get Stuck Next

After checking on the spare phone and shipping fixes, the next thing you'll likely hit is "I shipped it, but some users still see the bug." Most of the time it's the runtime version mismatch above, or users who haven't relaunched the app.

When you submit a native fix, rolling it out in stages rather than to everyone at once lets you catch the breakage the spare phone missed while it's still small. I described how I put that together in [A Phased Release Strategy for Rork Apps](/articles/rork-dev/rork-phased-release-staged-rollout-hotfix-strategy).

With the 27.3 beta under way, this is also when to decide whether the spare phone goes back on the beta track. Finish checking the store build on 27.2, then move the same phone to the beta and walk the same table from row 1 — repeating that loop is, I think, the lightest way to keep this going.

## One Thing for Today

Update the spare iPhone to 27.2, install your app from the store, and try rows 1 through 3. That's enough for today.

That unsettled feeling after a release gets a little smaller every year. With one spare phone, I can see things with my own eyes before I open the review section — and that order is the one I intend to keep.
