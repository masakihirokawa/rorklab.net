When I first asked myself seriously whether app revenue alone could pay for my life, I noticed something strange about the available reading material. There are mountains of technical books for app developers and almost nothing about the economics of running an indie practice. You can write code. But the income doesn't arrive evenly month to month, taxes show up as ugly surprises, health insurance feels punitive, and you have no idea when you can afford to upgrade your machine. Those aren't "app development questions" — they're "indie business economics questions."

I've been a full-time indie developer for twelve years. Some years my revenue dropped by half year-over-year. Other years it doubled unexpectedly. I'm writing this article to share, in full, the economic design that I learned through painful trial and error along the way. This isn't a technical article — it's an economics article. It will be especially useful for anyone considering going independent, or anyone two or three years into independent practice trying to figure out how to make this sustainable.

## What Indie Developer Income Actually Looks Like

Let's set the baseline honestly. "$10,000/month" in revenue sounds like more than double a typical office salary. Once you subtract everything that comes off the top, the takehome feels different.

A typical sole-proprietor expense structure in Japan looks like this (numbers in JPY, monthly equivalents from annual amounts):

```text
Example expenses on $10,000/month gross

National Health Insurance         ~$700
National Pension                  ~$170
Income tax + residential tax      ~$2,500 (set aside monthly for annual payment)
Business expenses (gear, internet, server, tools) ~$1,000
Net cash retained                 ~$5,630
```

After subtracting rent, food, and any family expenses, the takehome feel isn't dramatically different from a salaried employee earning $4,000–$5,000/month. This isn't to say "indie development doesn't pay" — it's to say that you have to know the gap between gross and net or your cash flow will eventually break.

In year three of going independent, I made the mistake of treating eight months of $8K/month revenue as evidence that I could safely take on a mortgage. The next year a store algorithm shift dropped my monthly revenue into the low $4K range, and the mortgage payment ate into operating cash. The iron rule: design your living costs against the trailing six-month average net, never the gross peak.

## Optimal Allocation Across the Three Channels

The three primary indie revenue channels are advertising (AdMob, etc.), in-app purchases (IAP), and subscriptions. Each behaves differently, so the optimal mix shifts as your business matures.

```text
Recommended allocation by life stage

[Phase 1] Right after going independent through year 2
Ads 70% / IAP 20% / Subscriptions 10%
→ Build cash flow first via DAU-driven advertising

[Phase 2] Stabilization, years 3–5
Ads 50% / IAP 20% / Subscriptions 30%
→ Grow subscriptions to raise the monthly floor

[Phase 3] Expansion, year 6+
Ads 30% / IAP 20% / Subscriptions 50%
→ Subscriptions are the foundation; ads and IAP are top-up
```

The reason this allocation matters is that each channel has a different "quality of variance." Ad revenue is the product of DAU and ad rates and shows large seasonality (especially in January, May, and the summer doldrums). IAP is one-shot and lumpy. Subscriptions accumulate and show much lower month-to-month variance, but take time to build.

In Phase 1, you center on ads because they generate cash immediately. Trying to build a subscription business from scratch can take 6–12 months to monetize, and your living expenses will run out in the meantime.

In Phase 2, you build subscriptions to create a stable floor. As long as monthly cancellations are less than monthly new signups, the base accumulates like a snowball.

In Phase 3, with subscriptions as the foundation, your monthly floor rises and you can invest long-term in new app development without the pressure of needing immediate revenue.

## Three Iron Rules of Cash Flow Design

For an indie developer trying to live off app income, monthly cash flow design is the most important discipline. Three rules I've held to for years.

The first rule: keep at least six months of living expenses in operating reserves at all times. My household runs at about $2,500/month, so I keep at least $15,000 in operating reserves. This is the buffer that says "if app revenue went to zero, I'd still survive six months." Twice in the past, this buffer is what saved me.

The second rule: hold tax and social insurance reserves in a separate account. Estimate annual tax burden roughly as 25–30% of revenue and transfer that share to a separate account each month. Without this, tax season will reliably arrive with "I don't have the money to pay."

The third rule: completely separate business and personal accounts. As a sole proprietor, these mix easily. The discipline of separation makes "what did I spend" and "what's left" instantly readable. All gear, Rork-related subscriptions, and similar costs come out of the business account.

```text
Account structure example

[A] Business deposit account
    Receives all AdMob, Apple, Google deposits
    Monthly: split into tax reserve, operating reserve, salary

[B] Tax and social insurance reserve account
    Monthly accruals for income, residential, health, pension
    Drawn down annually at tax filing

[C] Operating reserve account
    Holds at least 6 months of living expenses
    Tapped only in emergencies

[D] Living expense account
    Rent, food, utilities
    Fixed monthly transfer

[E] Investment / savings account
    Surplus funds, planned monthly contributions
```

Once I implemented this account structure, I was free of the recurring anxiety of "what if next month is short."

## Designing Around Seasonality

Indie revenue is more seasonal than most people expect. In my experience, the highest revenue month (December) and the lowest (May) often differ by 1.5–2x.

```text
Monthly revenue tendencies (long-run average for me)

January   90% (calm-down after New Year)
February  95%
March     100% (Japanese fiscal year-end)
April     85% (new-life setup, entertainment spend dips)
May       75% (post-holiday belt-tightening)
June      90%
July      100% (summer break begins)
August    110% (peak summer)
September 95% (back to school)
October   100%
November  105% (Black Friday spending)
December  130% (year-end shopping, breaks, downtime)
```

The wrong move is to budget annual revenue and divide by twelve. The right move is to design around the actual revenue curve, using the December–January peak to fund the May trough. Concretely: anything above living expenses in December and January gets allocated to the operating reserve specifically labeled for May.

Once subscriptions become your primary channel, this seasonality dampens significantly. Subscriptions are recurring billings, so monthly variance is much smaller than ad revenue. This is part of the economic case for building subscriptions in Phase 2.

## Preparing for Store Review Risk

The single biggest operational risk for an indie developer is app removal or account suspension by Apple or Google. When this happens, double-digit percentages of your revenue can vanish overnight. I've experienced it twice (once from a re-interpretation of guidelines, once from a third-party SDK behavior change).

Three preparations to make.

First, don't concentrate revenue in one or two apps. With ten or more apps spread across categories, losing one app caps the impact at under 10%. I deliberately structure my portfolio so the top five apps don't exceed 60% of revenue.

Second, deploy on both Apple and Google platforms. iOS-only or Android-only exposes you fully to that platform's risk. Pair Rork Max's SwiftUI native generation with a separate Android version (Flutter or React Native) for full coverage.

Third, monitor reviews monthly. When average rating starts dropping below 3.5, that's an early-warning signal of review risk. Apple tends to scrutinize lower-rated apps more aggressively.

## The Economics of Platform Fees

Apple and Google take 15–30% of revenue as platform fees. This isn't a fixed cost — it's revenue-linked, so the absolute amount grows as you scale. At $10K/month gross, you're paying $1,500–$3,000 per year per app to the platforms.

Two strategies I use that respect this fee structure.

First, run subscriptions through both Apple/Google and your own website. At the same headline price, the website channel keeps the platform fee as profit. The catch is that direct billing comes with credit card processing fees, tax compliance overhead, and customer support costs — so this only makes sense at scale.

Second, reinvest in Apple Search Ads and Google Ads strategically. Instead of paying the platform fee for organic discovery, you're paying directly for visibility — and at certain CAC levels this is cheaper than the implicit cost of weak organic visibility.

## What Changes Past Six Figures Monthly

Here's what most "make money with apps" articles don't tell you. Crossing the $10K/month line isn't the finish line. It's where the real economic challenges of indie life begin.

Health insurance shock comes first. National Health Insurance premiums are based on prior-year income. When my annual income crossed $120K, my health insurance jumped past $1,000/month the following year. This was one of the major reasons I started seriously considering incorporation.

Tax bracket jumps follow. In Japan, income tax rises to 33% above ¥9M annual income and to 40% above ¥18M. The intuition that "if I just earn a little more, things will get easier" is the opposite of the truth — earning more pushes you into harsher brackets. Another trigger for considering incorporation.

National pension alone makes retirement insufficient. Without the corporate pension that salaried employees receive, you have to combine iDeCo, small-business mutual aid, and Tsumitate NISA to build retirement savings yourself. Once monthly revenue exceeds $10K, contributing $500–$1,000 monthly across these vehicles becomes the standard life plan.

Incorporation timing comes from working with an accountant. Roughly: stable annual revenue above $120K, sustained business income above $80K, or planned capital investment / hiring all qualify as triggers to start the conversation.

## Realistic Advice for Aspiring Indies

Closing with the honest advice I'd give anyone considering going independent.

Save at least twelve months of living expenses before going independent. App development typically takes six months to a year before generating real revenue. "I'll be sustainable in six months" is optimistic — plan for one to two years of runway.

Make the transition gradual via side income first. Ship two or three apps while still employed; once side income is consistently $1,000–$2,000/month, then go independent. The psychological stability difference is dramatic.

Don't underestimate the health insurance and pension transition logistics. Within 30 days of leaving employment, you'll need to make 10+ decisions and submissions: National Health Insurance vs. employer continuation, national pension enrollment, residential tax payment method changes, etc. Have a checklist ready in advance.

For the first six months after going independent, minimize new app development and focus on optimizing existing app revenue. Right after going independent there's a strong urge to start something new, but stabilizing cash flow from existing assets is the safer first move.

## Reinvestment Priority for Gear and Subscriptions

Capital reinvestment for an indie developer should concentrate where it directly affects revenue. The priority order I've settled into over years.

Top priority is the development machine (your main Mac). A slow machine wastes hours per day. The latest Apple Silicon MacBook Pro is worth replacing every 2–3 years. This isn't depreciation — it's daily productivity.

Display environment is next. Dual or triple displays let you have code, tests, and store dashboards open simultaneously. Felt productivity rises 30%.

Development and operations subscriptions get curated by purpose. What I keep on continuous billing: Claude Pro, Rork Max, Figma, Notion, GitHub Pro, AdMob analytics, ASO tooling, RevenueCat for billing infrastructure. I review each annually for "monthly cost × annual cost × actual benefit" and cut anything that doesn't earn its keep.

Test devices for review submission also matter. Beyond the latest iPhone and iPad, keep at least one device from three or four generations back. This catches behavior on lower-spec devices and prevents review-rating crashes.

These gear and subscription costs run roughly $1,500–$2,500/month at the indie standard. Reinvesting 15–25% of monthly revenue into "business productivity" pays back over the long run.

## Treating Your Own Salary as a Decision

A sole proprietor is also the CEO of a company of one. Setting your own salary deliberately — not letting it just be "whatever's left" — is essential for long-term mental health.

Each month, I transfer a fixed "salary" amount from the business deposit account to the living expense account. Designating a "payday" gives life the same regularity it had in salaried employment.

I set the salary at roughly 60% of trailing 12-month average business income. The remaining 40% goes to taxes, social insurance, operating reserve top-ups, and business reinvestment. When business income grows, I don't immediately raise the salary — only when 3-month average growth exceeds 20%.

Implementing this freed me from the swing between "lots of revenue this month, let's splurge" and "low month, time to panic." This kind of self-managed compensation discipline is as important as any technical decision when you're working with variable income.
