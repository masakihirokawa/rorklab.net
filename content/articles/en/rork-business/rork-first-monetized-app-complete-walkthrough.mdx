I still remember the night I first tried to put an app of mine on a store. The build ran fine, but I hadn't touched the screenshots, the privacy policy URL, or the age-rating answers. That was when I noticed how far "the app works" is from "the app can be submitted."

After running apps on my own for a long time, here's what I'd say plainly: the wall is rarely the code. It's getting yourself to a state where you can finish the whole loop. Rork shortens the first half, building the app, quite a lot. The second half, from the store listing to actual revenue, still takes patient work whatever tool you use.

This piece is a map that strings the whole loop together. Not implementation details, but what you pass through, and in what order, before your first small payout.

## Make the First App Narrow, and Certain to Be Used

Early on, I aimed too wide. The more features I added, the blurrier it got who the app was for, and the results weren't great. These days my rule is: **the first app should be something one specific kind of person will definitely use.**

A few examples of narrow:

- A small helper for one profession (an appointment log, a freelancer's time tracker)
- A tool for a niche hobby (a vegetable-garden journal, a hand-efficiency calculator)
- Support for one everyday situation (a medication log, a once-a-week budget)

Don't chase "useful to everyone." Chase "the only thing this person needs." Rork turns plain-language ideas into a working base quickly, so it suits prototyping these narrow needs.

The question I use: "Would a few people around me really want this?" When family, friends, or coworkers say "I'd want that," there are probably others out there with the same pain. That's a hunch, not a guarantee.

## Thirty Minutes on Paper Before You Ask Rork

I wouldn't throw an idea at Rork cold, at least at first. Thirty minutes of sorting things out by hand cuts a lot of rework later. Decide three things.

**1. A screen list (five or fewer).**
The more screens you add, the lower the odds you reach the end. Draw five rectangles and write a screen name and its main element in each.

**2. The one action the app is really about.**
What do people do most often? It should finish within seconds of launch. For a budget app it's "enter an amount and save"; for a task app, "add a task." The feel of that single path is the core of the app's value.

**3. The smallest shape for data.**
Skip cloud sync at first. Keep data on the device, and add sync once someone is actually using it. Keeping that split in mind from the start avoids carrying complexity you don't need yet.

Those thirty minutes visibly change the quality of the prompt you give Rork.

## The First Hour: A Skeleton, Then One Fix at a Time

Once you're ready, ask Rork for a skeleton. A prompt shape I use:

```
Build a home-gardening journal app.
Five screens:
1. Home (today's tasks)
2. Plants list
3. Plant detail (watering + harvest log)
4. Add task
5. Settings

Top action: "add a task in a few seconds."
Data: local-only (local storage).
Design: bright green, readable typography.
```

Putting those five points in the first prompt (what to build, the screens, the top action, the data policy, the design direction) tends to return a skeleton you can actually build on.

Then touch it, and fix what feels off one thing at a time. **Don't ask for several changes at once.** "Change this button's color" and "add sorting to this list" as separate prompts make accidental side effects less likely.

If navigation works and the top action runs after about an hour, that's enough for a first target.

## Let Someone Touch It at 30% Done

When the skeleton exists, show it to people close to you while it's still rough. Waiting until it's finished is too late, and you won't have the energy left to change it. Ask only three things:

- Where did you hesitate first?
- What did you expect to be there but wasn't?
- What would you never touch?

The first question matters most. Where a person hesitates first is almost always a design problem on your side, and fixing just that changes how the app feels.

At this stage, favor feedback that says "take something out." Adding is always possible later; removing gets harder the more finished the app is.

## What the Stores Will Ask For

Once the app runs, it's time to prepare for release. Many people stall here, but a list written down in advance makes it a series of small steps. Fees, screenshot sizes, and requirements get revised, so check each store's current official guidance right before you submit.

For iOS (App Store), prepare:

- Apple Developer Program enrollment (the annual fee is in US dollars; the yen amount moves with exchange rates)
- App icon (1024×1024 plus the required sizes)
- Screenshots (follow the current device sizes in App Store Connect)
- A privacy policy URL
- Age rating answers
- The advertising identifier (IDFA) declaration
- Submission through App Store Connect, and the review wait

For Android (Google Play), prepare:

- Google Play Console registration (a one-time fee)
- App icon and feature graphic
- Screenshots
- A privacy policy URL
- Content rating answers
- The Data safety form
- A signed release build, and the review wait

Android has one more wall that's easy to miss. If you created a new personal developer account, you need a closed test with a set number of testers for a set period before you can publish to production. I sorted out how the count works when a tester drops out in [one of twelve Google Play testers left this morning: do the 14 days restart?](/articles/rork-basics/google-play-12-testers-one-drops-out-14-days-restart-or-not). Counting back from your release date and starting early would help.

The privacy policy is the one people skimp on, and it's required. If your app's data handling is simple, a practical route is to ask an AI like Claude for a draft, then add what your app really collects (including what your ad SDK gathers). I don't ship the template as is. **I check by eye, at the end, that what the policy says matches what the app actually does.**

## Preempt What Stalls Review

A rejection on the first try is quietly discouraging. Before your stomach sinks, check the usual causes.

Common iOS issues:

- **Too thin a feature set.** A one- or two-screen app, or a plain web page in a wrapper, tends to stall. Even with Rork, give the app at least one value of its own.
- **Crashes in a specific environment.** Test on more than one device size. If you claim iPad support, verify on an iPad.
- **Privacy disclosure that doesn't match.** Saying "we collect nothing" while an ad SDK collects data. Write down what your dependencies collect, too.

Common Android issues:

- **An outdated target SDK.** Requirements rise every year; check the current baseline before you submit.
- **Too little explanation for background location.** If you use it, say why.
- **Permissions you don't use.** Remove them.

Review comments are specific. Take them at face value and fix them, and later rounds usually go better, so one rejection is no reason to panic. If you're unsure how to place your release date, [when the review queue is unreadable, set your launch date outside it](/articles/rork-basics/rork-release-date-outside-the-review-queue) may help.

## First Revenue: Options and Their Order

The road from launch to your first payout depends heavily on what you pick. For a first app, this is the order I think is realistic.

**1. AdMob banner ads.**
The lowest barrier; some revenue starts even without many installs. Put the banner where it doesn't damage the main experience. How long it takes to reach a first payout varies a lot by user count, country, and genre, so I won't give a timeline. Start by watching your own daily numbers.

**2. Rewarded video.**
Tied to something like "unlock a feature once," it can raise revenue without breaking the experience. It costs a little more implementation work.

**3. In-app purchase (one-time or subscription).**
In a first app, the complexity of billing logic can pull quality away from the app itself. I'd consider it from the second app on. When you do add it, a personal developer may also need legal disclosure pages in some regions; in Japan that's the Specified Commercial Transactions notice, which I covered in [how to handle your address and phone number on the notice, before you add paid plans](/articles/rork-business/tokushoho-notation-before-paid-plan-address-phone-decision).

**4. Paid download.**
Charging at download time tends to struggle in casual categories, so I don't recommend it for a first app.

For the first app, **one AdMob banner and nothing else** is the version least likely to make you quit. Keep monetization simple and spend your energy on getting the app in front of people.

## The First Month: Check Daily, Fix One Thing Weekly

Launching is not the end. How you spend the first month shapes what comes next. What I keep doing right after launch:

- **Open the analytics every day.** In App Store Connect and Play Console, get a feel for installs, crashes, and drop-off.
- **Check how many people come back the next day.** Benchmarks differ by category, so I compare against my own previous week rather than other apps.
- **Reply to every review.** Thank the kind ones; answer the harsh ones with a concrete improvement.
- **Ship one small fix a week.** An app that keeps updating feels alive to the people using it.

Keep that up for a month and the app starts to feel alive. If updates stop within a few weeks of launch, it gets buried fast, so this is the one thing to keep up on purpose.

## Next Step: After the First Payout

That's the full path to the first payout. The real learning starts once money arrives: which ad placement works, who actually pays, when to show a purchase prompt. Those decisions are the next hill.

For choosing among models after that, I put the comparison in [Comparing Rork app revenue models: ads, subscriptions, and one-time purchases](/articles/rork-business/rork-app-revenue-model-comparison-ads-subscription-onetime).

**Your first payout depends less on how many features you build than on how many steps of the loop you complete.** I only noticed that after shipping a few apps myself. If you're not sure where to start, I'd begin with one thing: get a privacy policy URL ready.
