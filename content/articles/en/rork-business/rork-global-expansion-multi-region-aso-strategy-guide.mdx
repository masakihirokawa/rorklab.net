## I Left My English Listing as a Straight Translation for Years

Downloads from outside Japan had been creeping up for a while before I finally opened my own English listing again. The description was the Japanese copy, sentence for sentence, rendered into English. The keyword field was the same: every term that worked in Japanese, translated one by one and lined up.

I had never once checked what people actually type in that language when they look for an app like mine.

When multi-region expansion stalls, translation quality is rarely the reason. Far more often, the home-market structure simply gets copied sideways — the same discovery assumptions, the same price ladder, the same support expectations — without anyone asking what people in that region search for, what feels payable to them, or what makes them hesitate.

What follows is the order in which I'd open regions for an app built with Rork: how to cut regional blocks, keywords and metadata, localization past translation, pricing, compliance, and a two-week test sheet that tells you whether to move on. The idea is not to ship everywhere at once, but to open one region, check what happened, and only then take the next step.

One note on the numbers. App Store Connect behavior and legal penalty figures here were checked against primary sources. I have deliberately left out ad-spend benchmarks and "the right price for region X" figures — they swing too much by category, season, and region to be useful as a rule.

## Regional Blocks and the Order to Open Them

Looking at the world country by country leaves you frozen. I group countries whose search habits, willingness to pay, and regulatory weight are similar into five blocks. Inside a block, keyword construction, screenshot messaging, and support channels can mostly reuse one template.

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Block</th><th>What to check first</th><th>Where people usually trip</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>East Asia (Taiwan, Korea, Hong Kong, etc.)</td><td>The terms people actually use in that language. Even among Chinese-character markets, Taiwan and Japan differ</td><td>Direct translation of the Japanese listing; politeness register; app names that are hard to read</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Southeast Asia</td><td>Device capability and network conditions: lightness, offline behavior, whether it's free to try</td><td>Treating "Southeast Asia" as one market when language and payment habits differ by country</td></tr>
    <tr><td>South Asia (India, etc.)</td><td>Price sensitivity and support for local payment methods</td><td>Assuming English alone is enough — local-language copy builds trust in some places</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Europe</td><td>Privacy explanation, clear billing terms, separate locales per language</td><td>Shipping one English listing; compliance work comes earlier here than elsewhere</td></tr>
    <tr><td>North America</td><td>Ratings and reviews, and whether screenshot one conveys the value</td><td>The toughest competition — if you open here first, a weak result is hard to diagnose</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

My rule for ordering is simple. **Test small in less competitive regions, build a yardstick, then move into the crowded ones.** Open with North America or Western Europe and a weak result tells you nothing: was it keywords, screenshots, or price? You have no way to separate them.

  <thead>
    <tr><th>Stage</th><th>What to do</th><th>Condition to move on</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>1. Research</td><td>Look through roughly ten top apps in the target region and note their titles, subtitles, and screenshot patterns</td><td>You have 20 or more candidate terms people might type</td></tr>
    <tr><td>2. Open small</td><td>Release in one or two regions with the locale properly built out</td><td>Two weeks of impressions and downloads are recorded</td></tr>
    <tr><td>3. Separate causes</td><td>Sort what moved and what didn't into keywords, screenshots, and price</td><td>You can name the one thing to change in the next region</td></tr>
    <tr><td>4. Widen</td><td>Repeat the same steps in the next block; regions with heavier regulation enter here</td><td>You hold a numeric baseline per region (see below)</td></tr>
  </tbody>

## Multi-Region ASO — Rebuild Keywords Once per Language

App Store Optimization is how people find you in each market. A good app that can't be found in the searcher's language might as well not exist.

### Collect the Words People Really Type

The basic rule: don't translate, use the phrasing that is actually searched in that language.

If you're targeting people who search for a digital business card in Japanese, the English side could be "digital business card," "e-card," or "contact card app." The right answer differs by region, and even US and UK English can favor different words.

How to pack the keyword field itself is covered in the Japanese-locale article ([Maximizing the App Store Keyword Field in 100 Characters for a Rork App](/articles/rork-business/rork-app-store-keyword-field-japanese-aso-guide)). This piece is about preparing that design again for every language you add. For how keyword management looks in practice across many languages, see also [Global ASO Across 16 Languages](/articles/rork-business/global-aso-16-languages-strategy).

This order has caused me the least rework:

1. In the target storefront, write down the names and subtitles of ten competing apps.
2. Type seed terms into the App Store search box and record the suggestions that appear — they are a clue to what is really being typed.
3. Read a few dozen local-language reviews and note how users name the feature.
4. Rank the candidates on three axes — demand, competition, and relevance to your app — and fill the field from the top.

If you want numbers for demand and competition, ASO tools such as Sensor Tower can help. Treat them as estimates, though. The final call is better made with your own app's numbers, using the test sheet at the end.

### Which Fields Actually Affect Search

Confusing the fields wastes good local copy. Here is the working summary:

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Field</th><th>Limit</th><th>How to use it</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>App name</td><td>30 characters</td><td>Searchable. You can set a different name per locale</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Subtitle</td><td>30 characters</td><td>Searchable. Use it for the second keyword you couldn't fit in the name</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Keywords</td><td>100 characters, comma-separated</td><td>The most important searchable field. Words already in the name or subtitle are wasted here</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Promotional text</td><td>170 characters</td><td>A message you can change without releasing an update. Not used for search</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Description</td><td>4,000 characters</td><td>For convincing the reader. Generally understood not to affect iOS search ranking</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

One common mix-up: the 170-character field is promotional text, not a caption attached to screenshots. Text in screenshots is something you draw into the image yourself.

Also, a list of different words reaches more searches than a field stuffed with singular, plural, and near-synonym forms of the same word. Skip spaces and separate with commas only.

### A Starting Hypothesis for Each Block

Every region gets the same process, but the emphasis shifts. These are the axes I keep in mind:

- In East Asia, longer combinations that name a concrete use ("for sales teams," "free") tend to catch more than generic terms.
- In Southeast Asia, "offline," "lightweight," and "free to try" seem to get read before feature counts.
- In South Asia, put free access and the payment methods you support where people see them early.
- In Europe, privacy and clear billing terms shape trust more than a feature list. Build a separate locale per language, such as German and French.
- In North America, ratings, and whether the first screenshot lands its value, decide a lot.

These are hypotheses to start from. The test sheet later checks them one at a time.

## Localization — What's Left After Translation

Translation swaps one language for another. Localization asks how a reader in that language will feel on reading it and what they want in that moment, then reshapes the message.

"Save time" is one value, but for a working professional in Japan it may land as "streamlined work," and for a student in India as "shorter study time."

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Block</th><th>Tone</th><th>Presentation</th><th>Building trust</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>East Asia</td><td>Polite; avoid blunt translations</td><td>Clean, refined design with room to breathe; say concretely how life gets easier</td><td>Use local scenes of use</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Southeast Asia</td><td>Friendly; skip stiff formality</td><td>Brighter colors; say plainly what the app does today</td><td>Show use with family and friends</td></tr>
    <tr><td>South Asia</td><td>Short, simple sentence structure</td><td>Lightweight images; lead with free and offline</td><td>Local-language copy and visible payment-method support</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Europe</td><td>Formal and open about everything</td><td>Spare, uncluttered design</td><td>State what is recorded, where it is stored, and who can see it</td></tr>
    <tr><td>North America</td><td>Direct; imperatives read naturally</td><td>Show results rather than explaining</td><td>Quote reviews; be clear about refunds and cancellation</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Colors carry cultural meaning, but this is hard to state with confidence. Generic claims like "this color is lucky in region X" are worth confirming with a native speaker before you act on them.

### Translate What Sits Outside the Main Screen Too

Sample data, templates, tutorials, and canned support replies need reworking per region, not just the UI. A Japanese release might want Japanese-style templates and local contact formats; other regions need samples that fit their business customs.

When text grows longer, whole screens can break. What happens when a language whose words stretch, such as German, meets very large text sizes is examined in [With Max Text Size and German, the Generated Screen Quietly Jams](/articles/rork-dev/rork-max-pseudolocalization-dynamic-type-layout-resilience). Checking the container's tolerance before translating cuts rework.

## Regional Pricing — Pick a Base Storefront, Override Only Where Needed

"$9.99 in the US, so ¥1,200 in Japan and ₹500 in India" — straight currency conversion rarely works. Purchasing power, competitor prices, and attitudes toward paying all differ by region.

For building price and billing in a single market, see [Making a Rork App Hit on the App Store — ASO, Pricing, and IAP in Practice](/articles/rork-business/rork-app-store-aso-pricing-iap-strategy). Here we look at what changes when you carry that design into regions with different purchasing power.

### How App Store Connect Pricing Works

App Store Connect now works from price points (up to about 800 steps) rather than the old price tiers. The essentials (see [Apple's price-setting help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-pricing/set-a-price) for details):

- You choose one base country or region and set its price. Apple never changes the price in your base storefront on its own.
- Apple generates prices for the other storefronts from that base. It periodically adjusts them for tax and exchange-rate changes, and notifies you before it does.
- You can override the price in any individual storefront. Once you do, Apple stops auto-adjusting it there, and tracking tax and exchange rates becomes your responsibility.

So start with automatic pricing, and **manage manually only the regions where purchasing power or competitor prices make you say "this one should be different."** That keeps effort and benefit in proportion.

### Deciding Whether to Override

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>What to look at</th><th>How to check</th><th>Rule of thumb</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Competitor prices</td><td>Open the paywalls of top apps in the same category, in that storefront</td><td>Is the generated price far from what competitors charge?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>How the price looks</td><td>Read it in local currency</td><td>Does it end in an awkward figure?</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Billing model</td><td>See whether monthly, annual, or one-time dominates there</td><td>Start from the model competitors already use</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Free trial</td><td>Match competitors and local habit on length</td><td>Is the amount after the trial stated clearly?</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

In Europe especially, state the post-trial price and renewal terms plainly on the paywall. Unclear display reads as a dark pattern, costs trust, and can become a regulatory problem.

## Running Multiple Regions in App Store Connect

More regions means many more things to manage. Settling the routine early makes later ASO easier.

### Add a Locale

Each locale has its own name, subtitle, keywords, description, promotional text, and screenshots. Roughly:

```
App Store Connect → your app → the version page
  → Add a language (from the language menu) → choose the language
    → Name / Subtitle / Promotional Text / Description / Keywords
    → Support URL / Privacy Policy URL (per language if you can)
    → Screenshots (made with text in that language)
```

Menu names and placement change as App Store Connect is updated. Locate the add-language control on your own screen first.

### Add Countries and Regions in Stages

Availability by country is managed separately from price. Rather than releasing worldwide at once, start with two or three regions and add more after you've read the numbers:

- Bugs and wording mistakes surface while the audience is still small.
- You can measure each region's response without mixing them together.
- Support volume stays within what you can handle.

### Make Screenshots per Language

Reusing English screenshots in every locale tells someone searching in their own language, on the first frame, that this isn't for them. Even just swapping the first screenshot to that language's text and that region's key value helps.

  <thead>
    <tr><th>Block</th><th>Screenshot 1 says</th><th>Later screenshots add</th></tr>
  </thead>

## Acquiring Users — Start From Each Region's Usual Places

Costs and results swing by region, category, and season, so instead of amounts I'll only give where I'd try first.

- East Asia: short video (TikTok, Instagram Reels) and search ads are easy to start with.
- Southeast Asia: Facebook and Instagram ads with local-language captions are the base. Messaging apps differ by country — LINE is widely used in Thailand, Zalo in Vietnam.
- South Asia: local-language how-to videos on YouTube seem to build trust.
- Europe: search ads and instructional videos, separated by language.
- North America: reviews and write-ups, and community launches such as Product Hunt.

I run things as an individual, so I rarely start with paid ads. I first tune what happens inside the store (ASO), then pick local communities or video for what that can't reach.

### Keeping People

Match push-notification wording to local manners — polite in Japanese, friendly in English. Too many notifications backfire, so start with a small number and adjust after watching the response.

## Regulation and Privacy — Heavier With Every Region

More regions means more law to follow. Privacy rules in particular have large consequences, and fixing a problem later costs more. I've limited this to what I could confirm in official information. I'm not a lawyer, so take actual decisions to a specialist.

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Regulation</th><th>Main requirements</th><th>Scale of penalty</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>GDPR (EU / EEA)</td><td>Consent, transparent processing, erasure rights, impact assessments where needed</td><td>Up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher</td></tr>
    <tr><td>CCPA / CPRA (California)</td><td>Right to know, right to delete, opt-out of sale or sharing, no discrimination for exercising rights</td><td>Thousands of dollars per violation (amounts are adjusted periodically)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Japan's amended APPI</td><td>Specify and announce purpose of use, proper acquisition, information to the individual for transfers to third parties abroad</td><td>Up to ¥100 million fine on a corporation that violates an order</td></tr>
    <tr><td>India's DPDP Act</td><td>Free, specific, informed consent; notice contents; handling of data-principal rights</td><td>Up to ₹250 crore for failing to keep reasonable security safeguards</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

On India: the DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, and the main obligations are being phased in. According to published timelines, the core duties — notice, consent, rights handling — apply from May 2027. Cross-border transfers are permitted by default unless the government notifies a list of restricted countries. You may have read that Indian users' data must sit on Indian servers; I couldn't confirm any blanket requirement like that in the rules as they stand now. Sector-specific rules, such as payment data, exist separately. Check the official text for the latest.

### Priority Order for Checks

Doing everything at once isn't realistic. This is the order I follow:

1. Prepare a privacy policy in each target language, itemizing what is collected, where it is stored, why, and how to request deletion.
2. Confirm the in-app consent flow. Data sent by ad and analytics SDKs counts too.
3. Confirm your backend can actually act on a deletion request.
4. Each time you add a feature, recheck whether it collects more data.

Even if your own code sends nothing, you're still asked about what an embedded SDK collects. The procedure for auditing the whole dependency chain before submission is in [Auditing the Privacy Manifest Across the SDK Chain in a Rork-Generated Expo App](/articles/rork-dev/rork-privacy-manifest-sdk-chain-pre-review-audit-workflow). The more regions you add, the more that check weighs.

## Multi-Region Support — Build Only What You Can Reach

Support quality feeds straight into ratings. But an individual or small team can't run 24-hour coverage in every region.

My line: **don't promise what you can't deliver.** Write "we reply within 24 hours" on the store page and miss it, and the reviews that follow work against you for a long time. So I decide a few things up front:

- Publish a response-time estimate you can really keep.
- Collect common questions into a per-language FAQ page and point the contact form to it.
- Start with email plus a form, and add channels once demand is visible.
- Record the questions that recur per region — "does it work offline?" in Southeast Asia, "where is my data stored?" in Europe — and put them at the top of the FAQ.

If you add an automated reply, always keep a path to a human for when it doesn't solve the problem. Letting automation be the whole answer leaves frustrated people nowhere to go.

## Measuring — Build a Baseline Per Region

What distorts multi-region decisions is comparing yourself to someone else's averages. Retention and conversion figures quoted as industry norms vary so much by category, price, and funnel that they usually don't apply to your app.

What counts is **using your own app's past numbers in that region as the baseline and watching the change.** Here are the metrics, by role:

  <thead>
    <tr><th>Metric</th><th>Where to see it</th><th>What it decides</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Impressions</td><td>App Analytics in App Store Connect</td><td>Whether changing keywords moved how often you're found</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Product page views → downloads</td><td>App Analytics in App Store Connect</td><td>Whether screenshots and copy land with locals</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Trial start rate and paid conversion</td><td>App Store Connect or your billing dashboard</td><td>Whether price and the way billing is shown fit</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Retention</td><td>Cohort analysis in your analytics (e.g. Firebase)</td><td>Whether users in that region stick</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Rating and review content</td><td>Ratings and reviews on the App Store</td><td>Whether translation errors, misunderstandings, or support complaints are appearing</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

### The Two-Week Test Sheet

Here is, as written, the routine I use when opening a new region:

1. **Pick exactly one thing to change.** For example, "replace the keyword field with terms people really search in that language." Don't touch screenshots or price.
2. **Record the numbers before the change.** Note the last two weeks of impressions, product page views, and downloads, per region.
3. **Change it and leave it for two weeks.** Touch something else midway and you can't tell what worked.
4. **Set after beside before.** If impressions moved, the keyword direction is right. If they didn't, the cause lies upstream — in screenshots or price.
5. **Write the result in one line and choose the one thing to change in the next region.**

Seasonality and shifts in Apple's own ranking mean one result never proves anything. Even so, writing down what you changed and what moved makes each later decision easier as the region count grows.

## Where to Start

Trying to line up everything covered here at once usually means nothing gets finished. Narrow the first move to one thing.

Pick one language locale you already ship, and replace its keyword field — not with translations of your Japanese terms, but with terms actually typed in that language. Change only that. Let it run two weeks and see whether that region's impressions move. If they do, the same routine is worth repeating in the next language; if not, suspect the screenshots next. Set it up so that either result tells you the next step.

**Before adding regions, get to where you can say, for one region, what to measure to decide the next move.** Adding regions and deepening a region are different jobs, and I find later decisions easier when I've gone deep in one place first — the sense of what to measure carries straight into the next region and shortens the start.
