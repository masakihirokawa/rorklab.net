Whenever I see "build apps with AI" marketing copy, I tense up a little. I've watched too many tools demo beautifully and then fall apart the moment I asked for something I actually wanted. The night I first opened Rork, my skepticism was louder than my curiosity.

For a while I fed it small ideas one at a time and read every line of what came back, half expecting it to break. What I slowly noticed was that Rork isn't magic. It's a tool that reliably saves effort when the job has the right shape. This is where I've ended up drawing the line, with no exaggeration in either direction.

## First, Where It Doesn't Fit

Reviews tend to open with strengths, but the regret I see people feel usually lives on the weak side. So I'll start there.

### 1. Adding Features to a Large Existing Codebase

Rork is strong at starting from nothing and noticeably weaker at extending code that has grown for years. Even when I pass the existing code as context, the output drifts from my conventions and I end up tidying it by hand.

In my experience, small projects benefit from Rork-assisted additions, while a mature codebase is faster to extend yourself. Where that crossover sits will differ for each project, and I'd rather you find it than trust my number.

### 2. Work With Tight Performance Budgets

Games, AR, video editing: anything where the frame budget is thin. The generated code is a fine starting point, but the last stretch of tuning is human work. For ordinary apps where performance isn't the star, the automatic optimizations were never what held me back.

### 3. Logic Tied to Specialized Knowledge and Regulation

Medicine, tax, legal analysis: places where a wrong answer touches someone's life. Rork's grasp here is shallow. You can prototype, but shipping needs expert review and heavy rework. I treat this as a boundary I decide on before I start.

## Where It Does Fit: Five Spots

With those caveats in place, here is where Rork earned its keep for me.

### 1. Idea Prototyping

"Would I actually use this?" gets an answer in half a day to a few days. It helps most with requirements that almost fit existing apps: a to-do app with a gratitude-journal slant, or a family schedule app with a simpler screen for kids.

### 2. A First Version for a Solo Project

For small apps, the Rork-generated base can simply keep running. If traffic grows enough to demand a rewrite, that growth is the proof the rewrite is worth it.

### 3. The First Showable Form in Client Work

Putting a running app in front of a client during requirements discussions makes the conversation concrete. Taps and transitions that a Figma mock can't convey land immediately.

### 4. Validation Before Integration

For payments or authentication, I try the flow in a separate small app first. When the experiment is over, I throw it away and the main codebase stays clean.

### 5. Reading Generated Code as Study Material

Want to see how OAuth is wired into React Native? Generate it, then read it. You get working reference code, which suits me better than a textbook.

## Feature Impressions (April 2026)

### What Worked Well

- **Readable code**: The structure is straightforward and easy to follow compared with earlier tools.
- **iOS and Android together**: Both platforms are considered from the start, so there's less cleanup for platform differences later.
- **Sensible default design**: Color, spacing, and type sizes look natural enough to show as they are.
- **The easy-to-forget parts**: Error handling, loading states, and empty states come included.

### What I'd Like Improved

- **Complex state management**: Once state gets tangled, the generated code turns verbose.
- **Uncommon libraries**: Bringing in an in-house SDK can produce APIs that don't exist.
- **Tests**: Plenty of them, but some don't exercise real logic.
- **Docs catching up**: New features sometimes seemed to lag behind in the official documentation.

## Cost Depends on How You Work

I won't quote prices here; they change, and the right question is how the plan fits your habits. I tend to build many prototypes in a busy month, and writing all of them by hand would eat a large block of days, so the cost felt recoverable. That math belongs to people who want more output, though. If you build one app a month, or mostly modify existing code, it may simply be more tool than you need.

## What I'd Tell My Earlier Self

If I could send a note back to the day I started, it would say four things.

**1. Treat the first month as a time to break things.** Skip the real client project at first and build a few small prototypes to learn its habits. You get there sooner that way.

**2. Write prompts as carefully as code.** "A to-do app" and "a shared shopping list for three or four people, with sync, offline-tolerant, product photos optional" return very different results.

**3. Read before you edit in the first 30 minutes.** If you don't understand the choices Rork made, you'll lose the thread of why a given piece of code exists.

**4. Try the companion tooling early.** Wherever a connection to your local setup exists, it made things easier than working only in the browser.

## Will I Keep Using It?

Yes, with a line drawn deliberately: Rork up to here, me from there.

**Hand over the making; keep the judgment and the responsibility.** That's the rule I work by now. For solo projects and prototypes it reliably gives time back. As the main engine for large production systems, I still think it's early.

If you're curious, spend a week in the free tier and see whether it fits your hand. Deciding on a paid plan after that is soon enough.
