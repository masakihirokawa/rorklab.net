## Decompose the $700/month gap into three multipliers

Trying one idea after another and reaching the end of the month with no idea which one worked — that is how monetization often starts. I have been in that spot too: the list of experiments kept growing while my ability to read the results lagged behind.

So I look at monthly revenue as a product: **new traffic × conversion rate × retention rate.** It is a plain multiplication, but it works as a map for deciding where to spend effort.

For a Rork-built app, the two multipliers an indie developer can move by hand are **conversion and retention.** New traffic depends on ASO and store visibility, which leaves less room for control. Conversion and retention respond to in-app copy, timing, pricing, and experience design. This guide stays away from ASO and focuses on those two.

One caveat up front: the percentages and amounts below are placeholders meant to show how to think. Please re-measure everything on your own app.

## Pricing test 1 — A first-month discount as a "seriousness filter"

The first pricing test that tends to be easy to run is a **first-month-only discount.** If the regular plan is $5/month, offer $2.50 for the first month. Treat it less as a price cut and more as a filter for users who intend to use the app seriously.

### Implementation pattern in Rork

For a Rork-template app, store-native billing (StoreKit / Google Play Billing) takes less effort than rolling your own Stripe integration. Register the regular plan and the first-month plan as separate products, show the discounted one to new users, and show the regular one to returning subscribers.

The detail worth getting right is to decide eligibility from a **stable user identifier**, not a flag stored on the device. A device-local flag lets people reinstall and claim the discount again. An anonymized hash tied to the Apple ID or Google account is a practical middle ground.

### How to evaluate the result

The metric is not first-month conversion. It is **3-month retention × revenue per user.** If conversion rises but three-month retention drops sharply, revenue has not actually grown.

Even with lower retention, a wider entrance can win in absolute numbers. If the entrance does not widen, the discount is simply lost revenue. My own line: **do not call it a win until three months have shown that conversion actually moved.**

## Pricing test 2 — Regional pricing matched to willingness to pay

Both App Store and Google Play offer suggested prices per region. Following them is the easy baseline. A step further is to set regional prices by hand to fit your audience.

The idea is to lower prices where purchasing power is lower, and keep or slightly raise them where it is higher. How far to move differs by region, so start with a few regions and compare both conversion and total revenue instead of cutting everywhere at once.

Mind currency movement. Revenue converted back to your home currency drifts from what you planned, so audit all regional prices on a schedule. Putting it on the calendar every six months or so makes it hard to forget.

## Pricing test 3 — Separate the "displayed price" from the "paid price"

The third move is how an annual plan is *displayed*. Showing "$49/year (about $4/month)" makes the plan feel cheaper than a monthly plan standing alone, which helps steer people toward annual billing.

Annual plans, however, tend to bring more cancellation and refund questions. Refund decisions rest with the store, so what the app can promise is limited to making it clear where to cancel. Put the renewal date and the cancellation route on the screen shown before purchase. A path users can find and use on their own is, I feel, the most dependable protection against angry reviews.

## Retention move 1 — Onboarding that lets users feel the reason to stay

For subscription apps, **onboarding is where effort pays back most.** Rork-generated apps often start with every feature wired up but no story about why a new user should keep going.

### What an effective onboarding looks like

Add these three steps in order.

First, **ask exactly one question about the user's goal at first launch.** "What do you want to achieve with English study?" or "Why are you starting a journal?" Once the goal is named, the rest of the app starts to look like it is for them. Every extra question increases drop-off, so keep it to one.

Second, **send push notifications on day 3, day 7, and day 14.** Skip the feature pitch and affirm the experience: "Remember why you started?", "Here is your first week", "Two weeks in."

Third, **show one personalized "tip for sticking with it" during week one.** If usage logs show the user opens the app mostly in the morning, a message such as "Fold it into your morning routine" plays to the strength of an AI-integrated app.

### Implementation note for Rork

Onboarding is more about experience design than code volume. After generating the template, place these three steps **before the subscription screen.** Putting them after means the paywall appears before the user has felt any reason to continue, which tends to lower conversion.

## Retention move 2 — Streaks with interruption tolerance

Streaks are a staple of habit apps, but resetting to zero after a single missed day disappoints users. Add a **streak freeze** instead: for example, up to two missed days a month do not break the streak.

### Minimum-viable implementation

Compare calendar dates in the device's local day rather than subtracting timestamps. With timestamps, a late-night session and the next morning's session may not count as one day apart.

```typescript
interface StreakState {
  current: number;
  lastActiveDate: string; // YYYY-MM-DD (device-local date)
  freezeCreditsRemaining: number; // reset at the start of each month
}

// Treat "YYYY-MM-DD" as midnight UTC so only the day difference remains
function dayIndex(date: string): number {
  const [y, m, d] = date.split("-").map(Number);
  return Math.floor(Date.UTC(y, m - 1, d) / 86_400_000);
}

function updateStreak(state: StreakState, today: string): StreakState {
  const diffDays = dayIndex(today) - dayIndex(state.lastActiveDate);

  if (diffDays <= 0) return state; // already counted today
  if (diffDays === 1) {
    return { ...state, current: state.current + 1, lastActiveDate: today };
  }
  if (diffDays === 2 && state.freezeCreditsRemaining > 0) {
    return {
      ...state,
      current: state.current + 1,
      lastActiveDate: today,
      freezeCreditsRemaining: state.freezeCreditsRemaining - 1,
    };
  }
  return { current: 1, lastActiveDate: today, freezeCreditsRemaining: state.freezeCreditsRemaining };
}
```

This only rescues a single missed day. Gaps of two or more days still reset, so if the freeze feels too stingy, widen the `diffDays` condition. Compare three-month retention before and after, and judge the effect with your own numbers.

## Retention move 3 — Weekly reviews that make value visible

The third retention move is a **weekly review screen.** Every Sunday evening, generate a summary of "this week's record", "comparison with last week", and "next week's goal".

Subscribers lean toward cancelling the moment they realize they paid for a month they did not use. A weekly reminder that they *are* using the app gets ahead of that thought.

With a Rork-built app, a simple setup is to hand the last seven days of usage logs to an AI model and let it draft the review text.

```typescript
async function generateWeeklyReview(env: Env, userId: string, logs: ActivityLog[]) {
  const summary = logs.map(l => `${l.date}: ${l.action}`).join("\n");
  const response = await callClaude(env, [
    {
      role: "user",
      content: `Write an encouraging weekly review for the user in under 150 words from this usage log.\n\n${summary}`,
    },
  ]);
  return response;
}
```

If the logs contain personal information, pass only the fields the summary needs. Splitting the work also helps: **numbers and facts come from your own logs, and only the wording comes from the AI**, which prevents text that contradicts the data. ## Churn defense 1 — Offer a downsell to people thinking of leaving

One move that works near the moment of cancellation is a **cheaper, lighter plan.** Offer "If price is the issue, you can switch to a $2.50/month lite plan."

Keep in mind that on iOS the cancellation itself happens in system settings, and the app cannot get in the way. So the design is not a screen that interrupts the cancel button; it is a **plan-change prompt** placed near the subscription-management area in your settings. Registering the lite plan in the same subscription group lets users switch within that group.

Make the lite plan a reduced version. If it has the same features at a lower price, existing users all move to it and revenue per user collapses. One possibility is to cap AI use at five times a day and show ads.

How much the downsell reduces cancellations depends heavily on how the plan is cut. Record your cancellation rate before launch, then check the change against your own numbers.

## Churn defense 2 — A pre-emptive email to dormant users

For users who have not opened the app in two weeks, send a short note before they cancel: "We noticed you have not been around lately. If something is in the way, just reply."

Rork templates do not ship with email sending, so you will need your own setup, for example Cloudflare Workers with Resend. Before sending, make sure there is an unsubscribe path and that you have confirmed you may email these users.

The reply rate is far from high. Still, the replies tend to become hints for the next update, which makes this a worthwhile small experiment.

## Churn defense 3 — Show what they accomplished, not what they will lose

"If you cancel, you will lose access to your data" works briefly and erodes trust over time. Showing **what you have done so far** is the more honest approach.

For example, on a screen where users review their plan: "In the last 30 days you opened the app for 42 hours in total. Your journal holds 87 entries. They stay readable after cancelling, but no new entries can be added." The numbers are illustrative; the point is to **lay out what stays and what ends, calmly.** I believe that is both the most honest and the most persuasive way to let someone decide.

## Closing

Rork speeds up building. In the monetization phase, though, what moves revenue is not a special trick for AI-built apps but three old levers: pricing, retention, and churn, polished steadily.

One rule I keep through every experiment: **record the number first, then ship one change at a time.**

For your own Rork app, choose the single move you are most clearly missing and put it in production within the next week.
